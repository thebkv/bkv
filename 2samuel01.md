# 2 SAMUEL 1

## 01 — TELL IT NOT IN GATH

## The Natural Reading

> "Love your enemies, bless them that curse you, do good to them that hate you." — Matthew 5:44

David is in Ziklag when a man arrives from Saul’s camp with his clothes torn and earth on his head. Israel has fled before the Philistines. Many of the people have fallen, and Saul and Jonathan are dead. The messenger says that Saul asked him to finish killing him. He brings Saul’s crown and armlet to David.

The previous chapter tells how Saul fell on his own sword and how his armourbearer saw that he was dead (1 Samuel 31:4–5). Here we hear the messenger’s account in his own words. David receives the royal insignia from a man who claims to have killed the king. The crown has come within reach while Israel is still burying its dead.

David tears his clothes. His men do the same. They mourn, weep, and fast until evening for Saul, for Jonathan, and for the people of Israel who fell by the sword. The news that clears David’s way to the throne is also the news that his people have suffered a terrible loss. He gives himself time to feel that loss before asking the messenger another question.

The man identifies himself as the son of an Amalekite stranger. David asks why he was willing to strike the LORD’s anointed and orders his death on the strength of his own testimony. Twice before, David had refused to kill Saul when he had the opportunity (1 Samuel 24 and 26). Even now, with the crown in front of him, he holds to the same judgment about a life he had no right to take.

David then gives Judah a lament for Saul and Jonathan. The messenger has brought him objects that can pass from one king to another. The lament gives the people words for what they have lost.

> "Tell it not in Gath, publish it not in the streets of Askelon." — 2 Samuel 1:20

David knows what the Philistines will make of the news. In their streets, Israel’s grief will become an occasion for rejoicing. In Judah, David remembers Saul’s courage and calls the daughters of Israel to weep for the king who clothed them in scarlet. Then he speaks directly of Jonathan, calling him his brother and grieving the affection he has lost. The same song has room for the man who pursued him and the friend who stood beside him.

The disciple can watch what happens in himself when someone who opposed him falls. Relief may come first. After a long season of injury, relief is understandable. The deeper question is what he does with the news. Does he carry it from place to place because someone else’s disgrace now makes him feel vindicated? Can he remember a good thing about the person without having to revise what happened between them?

David’s loss belongs to the whole people, and he speaks of it that way. Before he is shown ruling Judah, he is shown grieving with Judah. What he does with Saul’s memory is part of how we learn what kind of king he will be.

## The Savior Test

> "How are the mighty fallen, and the weapons of war perished!" — 2 Samuel 1:27

Jesus gives the disciple a command that reaches into the moment David is facing.

> "Love your enemies, bless them that curse you, do good to them that hate you." — Matthew 5:44

David had already shown restraint while Saul was alive and capable of harming him. The lament shows what happens when the danger is over. Saul can no longer pursue him, and David can say whatever he pleases about him. He uses that freedom to grieve openly and to remember what Israel received through Saul’s reign.

Jesus calls the disciple to practise that freedom while the other person is still alive. A man can tell the truth about what was done to him and still pray for the one who did it. The next time he is tempted to pass along news of that person’s humiliation, he can ask what the telling would serve. David gives Judah a song to mourn by. Jesus asks what our own words give the people who hear them.
