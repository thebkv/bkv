# ESTHER 05

## THE GOLDEN SCEPTRE

*The decision becomes an actual approach*

---

## BKV Insight

Esther 5 is about carrying a costly decision into the action it requires.

At the end of Chapter 4, Esther said she would go to the king even though entering his court without being called could cost her life.

Chapter 5 begins when she actually goes.

She puts on her royal clothing, walks into the inner court, and stands where the king can see her. The danger she described in Chapter 4 is still real when she enters. Then Ahasuerus sees her and holds out the golden sceptre.

Esther is received.

This is an important stage for the disciple. You can know what faithfulness requires and even decide to do it while still standing outside the place where that decision becomes real. Eventually the decision has to become an action.

There is the conversation you actually have.

The truth you actually tell.

The thing you actually give up.

The place you actually go.

Esther said, “if I perish, I perish.” Now her feet carry that decision into the court.

Receiving the sceptre gives Esther access, but it does not finish the work. Haman still has his position. The decree against the Jews still stands. The king still does not know that Esther is Jewish, and Esther has not yet told him what Haman has done.

She now has access and begins using it.

Esther invites the king and Haman to a banquet. When the king asks for her request, she invites them to another banquet the following day. The story does not tell us exactly why Esther waits, so we do not need to invent her reason. We can see what the delay does.

It creates one more night.

Meanwhile, Haman leaves Esther's first banquet feeling honored. He has wealth, children, position, access to the king, and the unusual privilege of dining privately with the king and queen.

Then he sees Mordecai.

One man still refuses to stand for him, and suddenly everything else Haman possesses feels worthless.

> “Yet all this availeth me nothing, so long as I see Mordecai the Jew sitting at the king's gate.”

A single refusal nullifies all his abundance.

Haman appears powerful, but Mordecai still determines whether he can enjoy his own life. He has received almost everything his pride could ask for, yet one person who will not honor him is enough to poison all of it.

His friends and his wife Zeresh then help turn that grievance into a plan. A gallows is prepared, and Haman intends to ask the king for Mordecai's death in the morning.

Esther and Haman therefore move through the chapter in opposite directions.

Esther accepted that faithfulness might cost her life and walked into the place she feared.

Haman cannot accept that one man refuses to honor him and prepares that man's death.

By the end of the chapter, Esther has crossed the boundary that frightened her, while Haman has gone deeper into the resentment that rules him.

And while Haman prepares the gallows, neither of them knows what will happen during the night before the second banquet.

## Canonical History & Context

### From Decision to Approach

Chapter 5 begins exactly where Chapter 4 stopped.

Esther has told Mordecai:

> “I will go in unto the king, which is not according to the law: and if I perish, I perish.”

She then asked the Jews in Shushan to fast for three days.

Chapter 5 opens:

> “Now it came to pass on the third day”

Chapter 4 gives us Esther's decision.

Chapter 5 gives us the approach.

Keeping those stages separate helps us see what is happening. Esther has already accepted the possible cost. Now she enters the place where that decision will be tested.

### The Inner Court and the Golden Sceptre

Esther stands in the inner court of the king's house while Ahasuerus sits on his throne facing the entrance.

According to the law Esther described in Chapter 4, entering without being summoned can mean death. Her survival depends upon whether the king receives her.

When Ahasuerus sees Esther, she obtains favor in his sight and he extends the golden sceptre.

Esther touches its top.

The sceptre has a simple function in the story: **Esther's dangerous approach is received, and she is allowed to come near.**

It does not complete her mission. It gives her the access she needs to continue it.

### Banquets in Esther

Banquets have already played an important role in the book.

Chapter 1 opened with Ahasuerus displaying the wealth and glory of his kingdom through an enormous feast. Vashti also held a feast for the women.

Chapter 3 ended with another disturbing meal:

> “And the king and Haman sat down to drink; but the city Shushan was perplexed.”

Chapter 5 gives us a banquet arranged by Esther.

She chooses the guests. The king and Haman come at her invitation, and the banquet becomes the setting in which Esther begins moving toward her petition.

She does not reveal everything at the first feast. Instead, she asks the king and Haman to return the next day.

The narrator does not explain why she waits.

The delay nevertheless becomes part of the story's hidden providence because it creates the night of Chapter 6. Before Esther's second banquet, the king will be unable to sleep, the royal chronicles will be read, and Mordecai's forgotten service will be discovered.

### Mordecai the Jew and Haman the Agagite

The older canonical conflict remains in the background of Chapter 5.

Mordecai has been introduced as:

> “the son of Jair, the son of Shimei, the son of Kish, a Benjamite.”

Haman is repeatedly called:

> “the Agagite.”

These names recall 1 Samuel 15, where Saul, a Benjamite and son of Kish, confronted Agag king of Amalek.

Esther never explicitly says that Mordecai is descended from King Saul or that Haman is biologically descended from that particular Agag. Those claims go beyond what the text establishes.

The canonical correspondence itself remains striking:

**BENJAMIN / KISH → AGAG**

Chapter 5 brings that conflict back down to a very personal scale. Haman has received enormous authority, but Mordecai the Jew remains seated at the gate and refuses to give him the honor he demands.

The correspondence will become stronger in Chapter 9, when the Jews defeat those seeking their destruction and repeatedly refuse to take the spoil—the point at which Saul failed in his war against Amalek.

### The Gallows

The KJV describes Haman's proposed structure as a **gallows** fifty cubits high.

The Hebrew word is *ʿets*, the ordinary word for **tree** or **wood**. The execution intended in the Persian setting may have involved hanging or impalement rather than the later European form of gallows.

For Esther's story, its function is clear.

Haman has a structure prepared for Mordecai's death.

The structure he prepares for another man will soon become the place of his own death.

The reversal is already being prepared before Haman knows there will be one.

## Natural Reading

### 5:1–3 — Esther Stands in the Court

> “Now it came to pass on the third day, that Esther put on her royal apparel, and stood in the inner court of the king's house”

### What Happens

On the third day, Esther puts on her royal clothing and enters the inner court.

The king is sitting on his throne.

He sees Esther standing in the court, she obtains favor in his sight, and he holds out the golden sceptre.

Esther comes forward and touches the top of it.

The king then asks what she wants and tells her that her request can be granted even to half of the kingdom.

### The Key

Esther does exactly what she said she would do.

The danger described in Chapter 4 has not been removed before she enters. She crosses the boundary while the outcome is still unknown.

Then the king receives her.

### BKV

There is a difference between deciding to be faithful and taking the step that decision requires.

Chapter 4 brought Esther to the decision:

> “if I perish, I perish.”

Chapter 5 begins with her carrying that decision into the court.

The disciple reaches similar moments. You may already know what you need to do. You may have prayed, counted the cost, and settled the matter inwardly.

Then comes the actual conversation, confession, return, surrender, or act of obedience.

Esther enters without knowing whether the sceptre will be extended.

Then it is.

The sceptre means she has been received. The way forward is now open.

---

### 5:4–8 — The First Banquet

> “If it seem good unto the king, let the king and Haman come this day unto the banquet that I have prepared for him.”

### What Happens

Esther asks the king and Haman to come to a banquet she has prepared.

At the banquet of wine, the king again asks Esther what she wants and promises to grant her petition.

Esther asks the king and Haman to return for another banquet the following day.

She says:

> “to morrow I will do as the king hath said.”

Her full request remains undisclosed.

### The Key

Esther has gained the audience she risked her life to obtain, but she does not make her full request yet.

The story gives no explanation for the delay.

What we can see is what the delay produces.

There will be another night before Esther speaks.

### BKV

Courage does not require haste.

Esther has crossed the dangerous boundary, but she still moves one step at a time. She enters, she is received, she gives the invitation, and then she waits.

The disciple does not have to force the entire outcome once the first act of faithfulness has been taken.

Sometimes you do the thing that belongs to you and then allow the next part to arrive.

Esther cannot see what her delay is making possible.

Before the second banquet, something will happen that she could never have arranged herself.

---

### 5:9–13 — Haman Cannot Enjoy His Greatness

> “Then went Haman forth that day joyful and with a glad heart”

### What Happens

Haman leaves Esther's banquet happy.

Then he sees Mordecai at the king's gate.

Mordecai does not stand or move for him, and Haman becomes furious.

He restrains himself until he reaches home, where he gathers his friends and his wife Zeresh.

Haman tells them about his wealth, his many children, his promotions, and the authority the king has given him. He also tells them that Esther invited no one except the king and Haman to her banquet and has invited him again tomorrow.

Then he says:

> “Yet all this availeth me nothing, so long as I see Mordecai the Jew sitting at the king's gate.”

### The Key

Haman has almost everything he wants.

One refusal makes all of it feel worthless.

His happiness has become dependent upon receiving honor from everyone around him. Mordecai's refusal therefore carries more weight in Haman's mind than everything else he possesses.

### BKV

Chapter 3 showed resentment gaining authority.

Chapter 5 shows what living under that authority does to a person.

Haman has wealth, family, position, recognition, and access to the king. He has just been personally invited to dine with the king and queen.

Yet:

> “all this availeth me nothing”

A single refusal nullifies all his abundance.

This is what resentment can do inside the disciple. One injury, insult, refusal, disappointment, or person can take so much of your attention that everything else begins to disappear behind it.

The problem is no longer simply what happened.

The problem is that the offense has been given the power to decide whether you can have peace.

Haman rules throughout the empire.

Mordecai still rules Haman's happiness simply by sitting at the gate.

---

### 5:14 — The Gallows Are Prepared

> “Let a gallows be made of fifty cubits high”

### What Happens

Zeresh and Haman's friends advise him to build a gallows fifty cubits high.

In the morning, Haman can ask the king to have Mordecai put to death. Then, they tell him, he can go happily to Esther's banquet.

Haman likes the idea and orders the structure to be built.

### The Key

Counsel converts an emotional grievance into a scheduled, practical instrument of death.

Haman's anger now has a structure, a plan, and a time.

He expects to speak to the king in the morning and then enjoy Esther's banquet without Mordecai troubling him any longer.

### BKV

Resentment becomes more dangerous when it begins organizing the life around itself.

Haman no longer carries only an angry thought about Mordecai. He has begun building something because of it.

That can happen within the disciple as well. An unresolved offense can begin shaping conversations, decisions, relationships, imagined arguments, avoidance, and plans for what needs to happen before we will finally be satisfied.

Haman believes Mordecai's removal will give him peace.

Instead, the gallows show how completely the offense is already directing him.

He goes to sleep expecting to ask the king for Mordecai's death in the morning.

But the king will not sleep.

## Fractal Notes & References

### 1. Chapter 4 → Chapter 5 — Decision Becomes Approach

Chapter 4 ends with Esther saying:

> “I will go in unto the king.”

Chapter 5 begins:

> “Esther... stood in the inner court of the king's house.”

The story preserves two stages.

First Esther accepts what faithfulness may cost.

Then she actually crosses the boundary.

**DECISION → APPROACH → RECEPTION**

For the disciple, this is the movement from inward resolve into the action that resolve requires.

**Confidence: ESTABLISHED**

### 2. The Third Day

Esther enters the king's court:

> “on the third day”

The third day appears repeatedly across Scripture around moments of testing, deliverance, life, and decisive change. Abraham sees the place of Isaac's offering on the third day in Genesis 22. Hosea 6:2 speaks of being raised up on the third day. Jesus rises on the third day.

Esther itself does not explain the timing symbolically, so the correspondence should remain secondary.

Still, Esther moving from fasting under the threat of death into received access on the third day may belong to this larger canonical pattern.

**Confidence: PLAUSIBLE EXTENSION**

### 3. The Golden Sceptre — Approach Received

The golden sceptre has a clear function within Esther.

Esther approaches under the possibility of death.

The king extends the sceptre.

She lives and is allowed to come near.

The BKV function should stay close to that action:

**the feared approach is received and access is granted.**

Hebrews 4:16 later gives the disciple a much stronger and explicitly theological picture of approaching God's throne:

> “Let us therefore come boldly unto the throne of grace”

There is a possible correspondence in the movement of **approach → reception → access**, but Ahasuerus should not be turned into God or his sceptre into a fixed symbol of divine grace.

**Confidence: EXPLORATORY canonical correspondence**

### 4. Esther and Haman — Two Responses to Threat

Esther and Haman both face something they do not want.

Esther faces the possibility of death and accepts the risk in order to help her people.

Haman faces Mordecai's refusal and decides Mordecai must die so that Haman can be happy.

Their movements run in opposite directions.

Esther becomes willing to risk herself for others.

Haman becomes willing to destroy another person for himself.

**Confidence: STRONG RECONSTRUCTION**

### 5. Pride Before the Fall

Haman spends the chapter recounting his wealth, children, promotions, access to the king, and special invitation from Esther.

At the same time, his resentment toward Mordecai is driving him toward the act that will immediately precede his humiliation.

Proverbs gives the canonical pattern plainly:

> “Pride goeth before destruction, and an haughty spirit before a fall.”
>
> — Proverbs 16:18

Haman's rise has reached the point where he believes Mordecai can simply be removed.

Chapter 6 begins his fall.

**Confidence: STRONG canonical correspondence**

### 6. The Night Between the Banquets

Esther delays her full request until the following day.

That delay creates the night of Chapter 6.

During that night:

- the king cannot sleep,
- the chronicles are read,
- Mordecai's forgotten service is discovered,
- Haman arrives intending to request Mordecai's death,
- and Haman is instead commanded to honor him.

Esther could not have arranged that sequence herself.

The chapter therefore continues the book's larger pattern of hidden providence. Esther acts faithfully inside what she can control while events outside her control begin moving toward the same deliverance.

**Confidence: ESTABLISHED narrative structure**

### 7. The Gallows and the Reversal

Haman builds the gallows for Mordecai.

Esther 7 will place Haman himself upon it.

The instrument prepared for another person's destruction becomes part of the destruction of the one who prepared it.

Psalm 7 describes the same kind of reversal:

> “He made a pit, and digged it, and is fallen into the ditch which he made.”

Esther gives that pattern concrete narrative form.

**Confidence: STRONG canonical correspondence**

### Jew — יְהוּדִי (*Yehudi*)

Mordecai is again identified as:

> “Mordecai the Jew.”

*Yehudi* means **Jew or Judean** and is connected with *Yehudah*, Judah. By Esther's period, the word identifies the Jewish people more broadly. Mordecai himself has already been identified as a Benjamite.

Genesis associates the name Judah with Leah's words:

> “Now will I praise the LORD”

and with the Hebrew *yadah*, carrying the sense of praise, thanksgiving, or acknowledgment. The biblical wordplay is clear, while the exact ancient derivation of the name should be treated with appropriate caution.

For BKV, the historical identity gives us a clear disciple-level function:

**The Jew is the person who knows he belongs to God and lives from that belonging.**

That makes the contrast between Mordecai and Esther especially useful.

Mordecai is openly known as **“the Jew.”**

Esther is Jewish too, but her belonging remains hidden from the king.

Chapter 4 brought Esther to the point where that hidden belonging determined her decision.

Chapter 5 shows her acting on it.

## The Chapter in One Movement

Esther has already decided that staying faithful matters more than staying safe.

Now she walks into the court.

The king extends the golden sceptre, and the approach she feared becomes an open door. Esther begins using that access, bringing Haman into the setting where he will eventually be exposed.

Haman leaves the same banquet feeling honored, but the sight of Mordecai ruins everything for him. He has wealth, family, rank, royal favor, and the queen's invitation, yet one man who refuses to honor him makes all of it feel worthless.

His grievance then becomes a plan. The gallows are built, and Haman expects to ask for Mordecai's death in the morning.

By the end of the chapter, Esther has carried her decision into action while Haman has carried his resentment into action.

Esther prepares another banquet.

Haman prepares a gallows.

Then night falls.

The king cannot sleep.
