# 2 CHRONICLES 8

## 08 — THE KINGDOM BEYOND THE TEMPLE

Solomon has finished building the house of the Lord and his own house. Twenty years have passed, and the kingdom is entering a time of extraordinary prosperity. The Temple stands in Jerusalem, the sacrifices have begun, and the glory of God has filled the sanctuary. Now Solomon turns his attention to everything surrounding it. He builds cities, strengthens the defenses of the land, establishes places for storing provisions, and organizes the work of the kingdom.

The disciple should recognize this stage of the journey. There is a time when a man is occupied with seeking God and establishing a place for His presence in his life. But that is not the end of the work. The understanding he has received must begin to govern the rest of his affairs. His responsibilities, his habits, the way he uses his resources, and the work of his hands must all be brought into order. Solomon has built the Temple, but now he must govern the kingdom.

The chapter describes Solomon rebuilding cities and settling Israelites within them. He strengthens fortified places, builds store cities, and establishes places for his chariots and horsemen. His authority extends over the territory entrusted to him, and he is making preparations to preserve what has been established.

There is something the disciple can learn from this. Spiritual understanding must eventually become practical. A man may have experienced a genuine awakening, received an answer to prayer, or discovered something wonderful in Scripture, but he must still return to the ordinary responsibilities of his life. The question is whether he will carry that understanding into everything he does. It is possible to experience the presence of God in prayer and then conduct the rest of the day as though nothing has changed.

Solomon also organizes the labor of the kingdom. The remaining peoples of the land are assigned to forced labor, while the children of Israel serve as soldiers, officers, and commanders. The chronicler presents this arrangement as part of Solomon's administration, but the distinction deserves attention. The kingdom is becoming powerful, and much of its greatness depends upon the labor of others. The disciple should not overlook the people who bear the weight of that prosperity. A kingdom may be splendid to behold while placing heavy burdens upon those who sustain it. Later, when Solomon's son takes the throne, the people will complain about the grievous yoke his father laid upon them.

Then we come to one of the most revealing moments in the chapter. Solomon brings Pharaoh's daughter out of the city of David into the house he has built for her. He explains that his wife must not dwell in the house of David because the places where the Ark of the Lord has come are holy.

Solomon clearly recognizes the holiness associated with the Ark. He is careful about where Pharaoh's daughter may dwell, and he establishes a separate house for her. But this raises a searching question. Solomon is able to distinguish between a holy place and another place, yet how fully has he considered what it means to bring the whole of his life under God's rule?

The disciple should examine this carefully. There are things a man may readily bring before God, and other things he prefers to keep separate. He may pray over his work while refusing to examine his ambition. He may seek God's blessing upon his household while avoiding a difficult question about one of his relationships. He may be careful about the outward things he considers sacred while leaving other parts of his life largely untouched by what he knows of God.

The text does not tell us that Solomon's arrangement was made from an evil motive. It tells us that he considered the places associated with the Ark holy and acted accordingly. Yet the disciple can recognize the question that the arrangement raises. Is holiness something he reserves for particular places and occasions, or is he allowing the presence of God to influence the whole of his life?

The chapter then returns to the Temple. Solomon offers burnt offerings upon the altar according to the commands of Moses, observing the Sabbaths, new moons, and appointed feasts. He also establishes the courses of the priests and Levites according to the order appointed by David. The singers, gatekeepers, and servants are assigned their responsibilities, and the chronicler tells us that they do not depart from the king's command concerning these matters.

This is a picture of worship becoming part of the established rhythm of the kingdom. The Temple has not been built merely to stand as a magnificent monument. Its service must continue. The sacrifices are offered, the appointed times are observed, and the people entrusted with the work carry out their duties.

The disciple should understand the importance of continuing in what he has received. A wonderful experience of God may awaken him, but the life of faith must be lived afterward. Prayer, obedience, worship, and service become part of the ordinary course of his days. The disciple is learning to remain faithful when the excitement of the beginning has passed and the work must simply be done.

The chronicler then makes a striking statement:

> "Now all the work of Solomon was prepared unto the day of the foundation of the house of the LORD, and until it was finished. So the house of the LORD was perfected." — 2 Chronicles 8:16

The Temple is completed, and its service has been established. Solomon has accomplished the great work entrusted to him by David. What began as a pattern and a promise has become a visible house in Jerusalem.

Yet the story does not end there. Solomon travels to Eziongeber and Eloth, near the Red Sea, where Hiram supplies ships and experienced sailors. Together they bring gold from Ophir, adding still more wealth to Solomon's kingdom.

The chapter closes with the kingdom expanding into trade and prosperity. Solomon has built the Temple, organized worship, strengthened the cities, and extended his commercial reach. By outward appearances, everything is succeeding.

But the disciple should remember that outward success is only part of the story. Solomon's kingdom has reached a remarkable stage of development, yet the question of the king's own heart remains. The Temple is finished, but the man who built it must continue walking with God.

This is the lesson the disciple should carry from the chapter. He may accomplish great things, establish useful habits, build something beautiful, and receive many blessings. These are good things when rightly ordered. But he must continue to examine what is happening within him as his life becomes established.

Solomon has built a house for the Lord. Now the greater question is whether the life of the king will remain faithful to the God whose house he has built.

## The Savior Test

Jesus gives us a direct way to examine the distinction between outward religious accomplishment and the condition of the heart.

He says:

> "But seek ye first the kingdom of God, and his righteousness; and all these things shall be added unto you." — Matthew 6:33

Solomon has completed the Temple, established the worship of Israel, and begun extending his kingdom through cities, defenses, and trade. There is nothing inherently wrong with building, organizing, or prospering. But Jesus tells the disciple what must come first. The kingdom of God and His righteousness must govern the pursuit of everything else.

The disciple should consider how easily the order can be reversed. He may begin by seeking God, yet gradually become so occupied with maintaining what he has built that the work itself takes the place of the One for whom it was undertaken.

Jesus gives another warning:

> "For where your treasure is, there will your heart be also." — Matthew 6:21

The chapter ends with ships returning from Ophir carrying gold. Solomon's prosperity continues to increase, and the wealth of the kingdom becomes part of its greatness.

Jesus directs the disciple to examine what his growing prosperity is doing to his heart. The question is not simply whether he possesses wealth, but what place that wealth has come to occupy within him. Is he using what he has received in service to God, or has the desire to acquire and preserve it begun to govern his decisions?

Then Jesus addresses the question of holiness that arises in Solomon's treatment of Pharaoh's daughter:

> "Thou blind Pharisee, cleanse first that which is within the cup and platter, that the outside of them may be clean also." — Matthew 23:26

Solomon distinguishes between the places made holy by the presence of the Ark and the house where his wife may dwell. Jesus brings the disciple to a deeper examination. It is possible to be extremely careful about outward holiness while neglecting what is taking place within the heart.

The disciple must learn to bring his whole life before God. He cannot reserve one part for worship while allowing another part to remain governed by desires and assumptions he refuses to examine.

Jesus also says:

> "But the hour cometh, and now is, when the true worshippers shall worship the Father in spirit and in truth: for the Father seeketh such to worship him." — John 4:23

Solomon has established an extraordinary system of worship centered upon the Temple. The priests have their duties, the singers have their places, and the sacrifices follow the appointed order.

Jesus reveals the greater purpose toward which worship is moving. The Father seeks those who worship Him in spirit and in truth. The disciple may attend to outward observances, but his worship must become the genuine expression of a heart turned toward God.

And there is one further warning that should accompany every great accomplishment.

Jesus says:

> "For what shall it profit a man, if he shall gain the whole world, and lose his own soul?" — Mark 8:36

Solomon has built a magnificent kingdom. His authority is established, his cities are strengthened, and his wealth is increasing. Yet Jesus reminds the disciple that no amount of outward achievement can compensate for the loss of the life that matters most.

The Savior Test brings the chapter to its central question. A man may build a house for God, order his religious duties, govern his affairs successfully, and acquire great wealth. But is he still seeking the kingdom of God first?

The disciple should examine the life he is building. Are his daily responsibilities becoming expressions of what he has learned from Christ? Has his prosperity made him more generous and faithful, or more attached to what he owns? Is he keeping certain parts of his life separate from the truth he professes to believe?

Solomon finished the Temple, and the chronicler tells us that the house of the Lord was perfected.

Jesus teaches the disciple to look beyond the completed building and examine the heart of the man who built it.

The house has been finished. The life of faithfulness must continue.
