# PSALM 87

## 87 — BORN IN THE CITY OF GOD

The psalm begins with the foundation of a city. The Lord has established Zion upon the holy mountains, and he loves her gates more than all the dwellings of Jacob. Jerusalem was the place where God had chosen to put his name, and Zion became the center of Israel's worship. But the psalmist is about to describe a city whose inhabitants cannot be accounted for by ordinary ancestry. People from distant nations will be recognized as belonging to Zion, and the Lord himself will record their names.

The disciple should recognize the importance of this. A man may be born into a particular family, nation, or religious tradition, but the psalm points toward a belonging that comes from God. The city is precious because God has established it. Its security rests upon his presence, and its citizens are recognized by him. The question the disciple must consider is what it means to belong to the household God is building.

Then the psalmist declares, "Glorious things are spoken of thee, O city of God." What follows explains why the city is glorious.

The Lord names Rahab, a poetic name for Egypt, and Babylon among those who know him. He also names Philistia, Tyre, and Ethiopia. These are not simply neighboring villages whose inhabitants have always worshipped with Israel. Egypt had once held Israel in bondage. Babylon would become the great power associated with Jerusalem's destruction and exile. Philistia had long been an adversary, while Tyre and Ethiopia represented peoples beyond Israel's familiar household.

Yet the psalm speaks of these nations in connection with knowing God. The surprising declaration is that people associated with these foreign lands will be counted among those born in Zion.

This is a remarkable change in the way belonging is being described. A man who was born in Egypt does not cease to have been born there historically. But the psalm speaks of another recognition that comes from God. The Lord acknowledges him as belonging to Zion.

The disciple should consider what this reveals about the work of God. A person may have spent much of his life under influences that kept him far from the truth. He may have belonged to a way of thinking that opposed everything he now seeks. Yet the story of his former condition need not be the final account of who he is. God can bring a man into a new relationship with himself, giving him a place among those who belong to his city.

The psalm repeats the declaration:

> "And of Zion it shall be said, This and that man was born in her: and the highest himself shall establish her." — Psalm 87:5

Notice who establishes the city. It is the Most High himself. The security and permanence of Zion come from God, and the recognition of its citizens belongs to him.

The disciple cannot establish this belonging merely by claiming it for himself. Nor can another man confer it simply by declaring him worthy. The psalm describes something God recognizes and records. This is why the next verse is so important:

> "The LORD shall count, when he writeth up the people, that this man was born there." — Psalm 87:6

The Lord is keeping the register.

A human city may identify its citizens by family records, birthplace, or legal standing. The psalmist gives us the image of God himself recording those who belong to Zion. Their place in the city is acknowledged by the One who founded it.

There is something deeply reassuring here for the disciple. He may have come to God after years of wandering, and he may feel that others possess a spiritual history he can never recover. But the psalm invites him to consider what it means for God himself to recognize a man as belonging to his people. His past is real, but God is able to give him a new beginning.

The psalm ends with singers and musicians joining in a declaration:

> "All my springs are in thee." — Psalm 87:7

This is a beautiful conclusion. The city that God has established, the city in which the nations are counted as born, is now celebrated as the place of the singer's springs.

A spring is a source of water. It continues supplying what is needed for life. The psalmist has moved from the foundation of Zion to the source from which life is received.

The disciple should recognize the significance of this ending. He may spend much of his life looking outside himself for something that will finally satisfy him. He may seek security in possessions, recognition from other people, or a sense of belonging to a particular group. But the psalm points toward a deeper home, a life established in relationship with God.

The springs of Zion point the disciple toward the provision that comes from the Lord who dwells among his people. He is learning to receive his life from God rather than continually searching for another source.

Psalm 87 begins with the Lord establishing his city and ends with the people rejoicing in the life they find there. Between those two declarations, God recognizes people from nations that once seemed far outside the household of Israel.

The disciple is invited to consider whether he has begun to understand what it means to belong to God. His former identity, his ancestry, and his history may explain where he came from. But the Lord is calling him into a life in which he is known, received, and sustained by the One who established Zion.

## The Savior Test

The psalm makes an extraordinary declaration about the people of Zion:

> "This and that man was born in her: and the highest himself shall establish her." — Psalm 87:5

Jesus gives us the key to understanding the greater birth toward which this language points:

> "Except a man be born again, he cannot see the kingdom of God." — John 3:3

Jesus is speaking to Nicodemus, a ruler of the Jews who already possesses a place among the covenant people by natural birth. Yet Jesus tells him that another birth is necessary.

Nicodemus is confused because he thinks Jesus is speaking about returning to his mother's womb. Jesus explains:

> "Except a man be born of water and of the Spirit, he cannot enter into the kingdom of God." — John 3:5

Here is the connection the disciple should recognize. Psalm 87 celebrates people whom God acknowledges as born in Zion, even though they have come from other nations. Jesus reveals that entrance into the kingdom requires a birth of the Spirit.

The psalm speaks of belonging to the city of God. Jesus teaches how a man enters the kingdom of God.

The disciple must understand that he cannot rely merely upon the circumstances of his natural birth, his family history, or his religious inheritance. Jesus calls him into a new life brought forth by the Spirit.

There is another confirmation in the words of Jesus:

> "And other sheep I have, which are not of this fold: them also I must bring, and they shall hear my voice; and there shall be one fold, and one shepherd." — John 10:16

Psalm 87 names people from Egypt, Babylon, Philistia, Tyre, and Ethiopia who will be recognized in Zion. Jesus speaks of other sheep whom he will bring into one flock under one shepherd.

The disciple should recognize the work of the Savior here. Those who were far away are being gathered. Their former place among the nations does not prevent them from hearing the voice of Christ.

Jesus makes the promise even more explicit:

> "And I say unto you, That many shall come from the east and west, and shall sit down with Abraham, and Isaac, and Jacob, in the kingdom of heaven." — Matthew 8:11

The kingdom is receiving people from beyond the boundaries that many had assumed would determine belonging. Jesus confirms that the promise of God reaches those who come in faith.

This also helps us understand the image of the Lord recording the people of Zion. Jesus tells his disciples:

> "But rather rejoice, because your names are written in heaven." — Luke 10:20

The disciples have returned rejoicing because evil spirits were subject to them in Jesus' name. But Jesus redirects their attention. Their greatest cause for rejoicing is that their names are written in heaven.

This is remarkably close to the heart of Psalm 87. The psalmist celebrates the Lord recording those who belong to his city. Jesus tells his disciples to rejoice that heaven knows their names.

The disciple should consider the difference between seeking spiritual importance and finding his place with God. He may desire great knowledge, remarkable experiences, or some visible proof of spiritual accomplishment. But Jesus tells him that his greatest joy is found in belonging to the kingdom.

Then we come to the final verse:

> "As well the singers as the players on instruments shall be there: all my springs are in thee." — Psalm 87:7

Jesus speaks to the woman of Samaria about another source of water:

> "But whosoever drinketh of the water that I shall give him shall never thirst; but the water that I shall give him shall be in him a well of water springing up into everlasting life." — John 4:14

The psalm ends with the celebration of springs. Jesus reveals the living water that he gives, which becomes within the believer a well springing up into everlasting life.

The disciple should recognize what this means for his own search for satisfaction and belonging. He may have spent years looking for a place where he could finally feel secure, accepted, and complete. Jesus offers something deeper than membership in an earthly household. He gives the life of the Spirit and brings the disciple into fellowship with the Father.

The Savior Test reveals the remarkable movement of Psalm 87. God establishes Zion, recognizes people from distant nations as belonging to her, records their names, and becomes the source of their rejoicing.

Jesus confirms the greater fulfillment. A man must be born of the Spirit. The Shepherd gathers other sheep into his flock. People come from east and west into the kingdom. Their names are written in heaven, and Christ gives them living water.

The disciple should ask himself whether he is still relying upon the identity he received from the world or whether he has begun to live from the new birth Jesus describes. Does he seek his security in the approval and recognition of other people, or does he rejoice that he is known by God? And where does he turn for the life that satisfies his deepest need?

The nations are being gathered. The Lord is recording those who belong to his city.

And the singers rejoice because all their springs are there.
