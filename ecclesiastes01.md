# ECCLESIASTES 1

## 01 — THE KING WHO HAD EVERYTHING

Ecclesiastes opens with the words of the Preacher, the son of David, king in Jerusalem. He begins with a startling declaration: "Vanity of vanities, saith the Preacher, vanity of vanities; all is vanity."

The word translated *vanity* comes from the Hebrew *hebel*, meaning a breath or vapor. It describes something that appears for a moment and then passes away, something a man can see but cannot hold in his hands. The Preacher has been examining human life and has discovered that so much of what men spend their lives pursuing is like this. They labor to acquire possessions, establish reputations, gain knowledge, and build things they hope will endure. Yet time continues moving, circumstances change, and the things they thought were permanent begin to disappear.

Then he asks the question that governs the chapter: "What profit hath a man of all his labour which he taketh under the sun?"

The phrase *under the sun* is important. The Preacher is examining life from the standpoint of what can be observed in this world. He is looking at the work of men, the movement of nature, and the passage of time, trying to discover what lasting gain can be found in them.

The disciple should recognize this question. He may have spent years working toward something he believed would finally satisfy him. Perhaps he wanted a certain amount of money, a place of security, recognition for his abilities, or the freedom to arrange his life as he pleased. But after reaching one goal, another desire appeared. Even his accomplishments may have brought less satisfaction than he expected.

The Preacher then looks at the natural world. One generation passes away and another comes, but the earth continues. The sun rises, goes down, and returns to begin its course again. The wind travels south and turns north, continually moving through its circuits. The rivers run into the sea, yet the sea is never full, and the waters continue their course.

There is a remarkable observation here. The world is filled with movement, but the movement never seems to bring anything to completion. The sun rises only to set again. The rivers pour into the sea without filling it. The wind moves continually without arriving at a final destination.

The disciple should consider how closely this resembles a life governed entirely by outward desires. A man works to obtain something, enjoys it for a season, and then begins pursuing something else. He may become very busy and accomplish many things, yet still find himself returning to the same dissatisfaction that first sent him searching.

The Preacher says, "The eye is not satisfied with seeing, nor the ear filled with hearing."

Here the question moves from the natural world into the man himself. The eye continues looking, and the ear continues listening, because neither can receive enough of the world to satisfy the whole man. There is always another sight to see, another experience to enjoy, another thing to learn, and another possession to obtain.

The disciple may recognize this in the way his attention moves from one thing to another. He can spend an entire day receiving information, entertainment, and impressions, yet come to the end of it feeling strangely empty. The difficulty is not necessarily that anything he encountered was evil. It is that he was asking the things of the world to give him a satisfaction they were never able to provide.

Then the Preacher declares that what has been will be again, and what has been done will be done again. "There is no new thing under the sun."

He observes that even the things men celebrate as entirely new often repeat patterns that have appeared before. Generations forget what previous generations experienced, and each new generation imagines that its discoveries and struggles are unlike anything that has ever happened.

This is another warning for the disciple. He may believe that changing his circumstances will finally solve the problem of his dissatisfaction. A new occupation, a new possession, a new place to live, or a new achievement may seem to promise an entirely different life. Yet if the same desires and assumptions continue governing him, he may discover that he has carried the old life into the new circumstances.

The Preacher also observes that there is no lasting remembrance of former things. Even the achievements that seem remarkable in their own time are eventually forgotten.

A man may devote his life to becoming important in the eyes of others, but the world moves on. His name may be remembered for a while, or perhaps for many generations, yet even this cannot answer the deeper question of what his life has been for.

The chapter then turns to the Preacher's own investigation. He tells us that he gave his heart to seek and search out by wisdom concerning everything done under heaven. He was determined to understand the world and discover what could be gained from examining it.

This is important because the Preacher is not speaking from ignorance. He has applied his understanding to the problem. He has studied the works of men, observed their struggles, and increased his knowledge. Yet his investigation has brought him to a painful conclusion.

He has seen all the works done under the sun, and behold, all is vanity and vexation of spirit.

The expression translated *vexation of spirit* is often understood as striving after wind. The picture is of a man attempting to grasp something that continually escapes him. He may exert tremendous effort and even understand much of what he encounters, but he cannot take hold of the lasting satisfaction he seeks.

The Preacher then says, "That which is crooked cannot be made straight: and that which is wanting cannot be numbered."

He has discovered limitations that human wisdom cannot overcome simply by understanding them. There are things he cannot repair by examining them more carefully, and there are deficiencies he cannot supply through knowledge alone.

The disciple should pay attention to this distinction. Understanding a problem is valuable, but understanding does not necessarily give a man the power to overcome it. He may know exactly why he has become fearful, resentful, or dissatisfied, yet still find himself living under the authority of those conditions.

Finally, the Preacher tells us that he has acquired great wisdom, more than those who were before him in Jerusalem. He has given his heart to know wisdom, madness, and folly. Yet even this pursuit has become another striving after wind.

He concludes, "For in much wisdom is much grief: and he that increaseth knowledge increaseth sorrow."

This is a sobering ending. The more closely he examines the world, the more clearly he sees its limitations. Knowledge has increased his awareness of the problem without bringing him the answer he desires.

The disciple must understand what the chapter is showing him. The Preacher has examined the world with extraordinary intelligence, but he has been asking what lasting profit can be found *under the sun*. He has looked at the passing generations, the endless movement of nature, the hunger of the senses, and the accomplishments of men. Everywhere he finds movement, change, and impermanence.

Yet the very dissatisfaction he describes points toward something important. Why does man continue seeking a satisfaction that the visible world cannot provide? Why does he desire something that lasts when everything he observes is passing away?

The Preacher has not yet completed his investigation. This is only the first chapter. But he has already brought the disciple to an important discovery: the things of the world cannot bear the whole weight of his hope.

He must learn where the life he is seeking can actually be found.

## The Savior Test

Jesus gives us a remarkable confirmation of the Preacher's observation about human desire.

The Preacher says:

> "The eye is not satisfied with seeing, nor the ear filled with hearing." — Ecclesiastes 1:8

Jesus speaks to the woman at the well and tells her:

> "Whosoever drinketh of this water shall thirst again." — John 4:13

This is the experience Ecclesiastes has been describing. A man receives something from the world, but the satisfaction passes and the desire returns. The water is real, and it answers a genuine need, but the man must come back and drink again.

Jesus then tells the woman:

> "But whosoever drinketh of the water that I shall give him shall never thirst." — John 4:14

Here Jesus reveals something the Preacher has been searching for. There is a life that can satisfy the deeper hunger within man. It is not another possession, achievement, or experience added to everything he already has. Jesus offers living water that becomes within the person a well springing up into everlasting life.

The disciple should recognize the difference. The Preacher has been trying to discover what the world can give him. Jesus directs the disciple toward what God gives through his Son.

There is another extraordinary connection.

The Preacher tells us that he gave his heart to seek wisdom and understand everything done under heaven. He had increased his knowledge, but his investigation ended in grief.

Jesus speaks directly about Solomon when he says:

> "Behold, a greater than Solomon is here." — Matthew 12:42

Solomon became famous for his wisdom, and the queen of the south traveled a great distance to hear him. But Jesus tells his hearers that someone greater than Solomon is standing before them.

This is a crucial part of the Savior Test. The disciple may pursue wisdom, study the Scriptures, and increase his understanding of the world, but Jesus calls him to something greater than the wisdom of even the greatest earthly teacher. He calls him to know the Father through the Son.

Jesus also says:

> "And ye shall know the truth, and the truth shall make you free." — John 8:32

The Preacher has discovered that knowledge can increase sorrow. Jesus speaks of a truth that makes a man free. The disciple must learn the difference between accumulating information about life and receiving the truth that changes the way he lives.

The Preacher has also been troubled by the passing nature of everything he observes. Generations come and go, great works are forgotten, and nothing in the visible world seems able to endure.

Jesus tells us:

> "Heaven and earth shall pass away, but my words shall not pass away." — Matthew 24:35

Here Jesus answers the Preacher's longing for something that will last. The disciple can spend his life trying to preserve things that are continually changing, or he can begin building upon the Word that Jesus declares will endure.

This does not mean that the world God created is worthless. Jesus himself taught his disciples to recognize the Father's care in the birds of the air and the lilies of the field. The created world bears witness to God's goodness. But the disciple must learn to receive it without expecting it to become his eternal foundation.

Finally, Jesus gives an invitation to those who have become weary from their striving:

> "Come unto me, all ye that labour and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest." — Matthew 11:28

The Preacher has labored to understand the world and discover its lasting profit. His wisdom has brought him face to face with the limits of what he can accomplish through his own efforts.

Jesus invites the weary man to come to him.

The disciple should ask himself what he has been pursuing in the hope that it would finally satisfy him. Has he been looking for security in possessions, meaning in recognition, or peace in knowing enough to control his circumstances? Has he mistaken constant activity for genuine progress?

The Savior Test brings the opening chapter of Ecclesiastes into focus. The Preacher shows us the weariness of a life searching for lasting satisfaction under the sun. Jesus reveals the living water, the enduring Word, and the rest that comes from him.

The disciple may continue working, learning, building, and enjoying the good things God has provided. But he is no longer required to seek his deepest satisfaction in things that continually pass away.

The King has examined everything under the sun.

Jesus invites the disciple to receive the life that comes from the Father.
