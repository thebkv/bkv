# JOEL 2

## 02 — THE YEARS THE LOCUSTS ATE

## The Natural Reading

> "Repent ye: for the kingdom of heaven is at hand." — Matthew 3:2

Joel begins with the sound of a trumpet in Zion. The alarm is to be raised upon God's holy mountain because the day of the Lord is coming. The prophet describes a day of darkness and gloominess, with clouds and thick darkness covering the land. A great and powerful army advances, spreading destruction wherever it goes. Before it lies a land like the garden of Eden, but behind it remains a desolate wilderness. Nothing seems able to escape its advance. The people tremble, the earth shakes, and even the sun and moon are darkened in the prophet's vision.

The disciple should understand that Joel is continuing the warning begun in the first chapter. The locusts had already devastated the harvest, but now the prophet describes the approaching judgment with the language of an invading army. The creatures move in ranks, climb the walls, enter the houses, and overwhelm every defense. Whether we consider the locust imagery itself or the military threat it also evokes, the picture is of something the people cannot stop by their own strength. They are being confronted with the consequences of a condition that has gone far beyond their ability to control.

Then Joel asks a question that brings the whole scene into focus: "For the day of the LORD is great and very terrible; and who can abide it?" The people have been looking at the destruction around them, but the prophet directs their attention to the Lord before whom they must stand. Their greatest need is not simply to recover the fields or defeat an approaching enemy. They must return to God.

At this point, the prophecy changes. In the middle of the warning, the Lord speaks:

> "Therefore also now, saith the LORD, turn ye even to me with all your heart, and with fasting, and with weeping, and with mourning." — Joel 2:12

The word *now* is important. The people have already suffered terrible losses, and the warning of further judgment is before them. Yet God is still calling them to return. They have not been told that their opportunity has passed. The disciple should recognize the mercy in this invitation. A man may have followed the wrong course for a long time, allowing pride, fear, resentment, or some other desire to govern him. He may already be living with the consequences. But the Lord calls him to turn from the way he has been walking and come back to Him.

Joel then gives an instruction that reaches beneath every outward expression of repentance:

> "And rend your heart, and not your garments, and turn unto the LORD your God: for he is gracious and merciful, slow to anger, and of great kindness, and repenteth him of the evil." — Joel 2:13

In Israel, tearing one's garments was a visible sign of grief and distress. Joel tells the people that God requires something deeper. They must allow the sorrow they express outwardly to become a genuine turning within. They cannot simply perform the ceremonies of repentance while continuing to cherish the things that have drawn them away. The disciple should examine this carefully. He may admit that he has done wrong, feel genuine sorrow over the consequences, and even make promises to change. But has he become willing to surrender the thinking and desires that brought him into the condition in the first place? Joel is calling for the heart itself to turn toward God.

The prophet then commands the trumpet to be blown again, but this time the people are to gather for a solemn assembly. The elders, children, nursing infants, bridegroom, and bride are all summoned. The priests are to weep between the porch and the altar, asking the Lord to spare His people. The entire nation must recognize its dependence upon God. No one is too important to be called, and no one is excluded because of age or ordinary responsibilities. The people are being gathered to seek the Lord together.

Then comes the answer. Joel declares that the Lord will be jealous for His land and pity His people. He promises grain, wine, and oil, and says that they will be satisfied. The northern threat will be removed, and the land that had been devastated will become fruitful again. The pastures of the wilderness will spring up, the trees will bear fruit, and the fig tree and vine will yield their strength.

The disciple should notice how the prophecy moves from repentance to restoration. The people had been living under the consequences of loss, but God now promises to provide for them again. The fields that seemed beyond recovery will become fruitful. The land itself is called to rejoice because the Lord has done great things. This does not mean that every loss in a disciple's life will be restored in precisely the form it once possessed. Joel is speaking a promise to Israel concerning a devastated land. But the disciple can recognize the character of the God who calls His people back. The Lord's purpose includes mercy, renewal, and the restoration of life.

Then God gives one of the most remarkable promises in the chapter:

> "And I will restore to you the years that the locust hath eaten, the cankerworm, and the caterpiller, and the palmerworm, my great army which I sent among you." — Joel 2:25

This is much more than the promise of another harvest. The people have lost years of fruitfulness, labor, and provision. The locusts consumed what had been growing, and the consequences reached into every part of their lives. Now God speaks of restoring the years themselves.

The disciple should consider what this means without losing sight of the actual promise. A man cannot return to yesterday and live it again. He cannot recover every opportunity exactly as it once existed. But God is able to bring fruitfulness out of a life that has suffered great loss. A man may have spent years following the wrong desires or resisting what he knew was right. When he returns to God, he may discover that those years have taught him something he could never have learned while defending his former way. The Lord can bring new life, wisdom, and usefulness where the man had begun to believe that everything was wasted.

The people are promised that they will eat in plenty, be satisfied, and praise the name of the Lord who has dealt wondrously with them. They will know that the Lord is in the midst of Israel and that there is none else. This is the greater restoration toward which the promise leads. The harvest returns, but the people must also come to recognize the One who gives it. They are being brought back into the knowledge of God's presence among them.

Then Joel looks beyond the restored fields to a promise that reaches into the New Testament:

> "And it shall come to pass afterward, that I will pour out my spirit upon all flesh; and your sons and your daughters shall prophesy, your old men shall dream dreams, your young men shall see visions." — Joel 2:28

The Spirit of God will be poured out upon all flesh. Sons and daughters, old men and young men, servants and handmaids are named as recipients. The promise extends beyond the familiar distinctions of age, sex, and social position. God's Spirit will be given in a way that reaches through the whole community.

This is an extraordinary enlargement of the promise. Joel began with a people who had lost the fruit of their fields. Now he speaks of the Spirit of God being poured out upon them. The disciple should recognize the progression. God promises to restore outward provision, but He also promises something far greater: His own Spirit. The people who had been called to return with their whole hearts are now shown the life God intends to pour out upon them.

Joel then speaks of wonders in heaven and earth, blood, fire, and pillars of smoke. The sun will be turned into darkness and the moon into blood before the great and terrible day of the Lord. These prophetic signs remind the hearer that the day of the Lord remains a matter of profound seriousness. But the chapter closes with a promise of deliverance:

> "And it shall come to pass, that whosoever shall call on the name of the LORD shall be delivered." — Joel 2:32

The word *whosoever* deserves attention. After the terrible warnings, the destruction, the call to repentance, and the promise of the Spirit, the Lord declares that those who call upon His name shall find deliverance. Joel speaks of this deliverance in Mount Zion and Jerusalem, and among the remnant whom the Lord shall call.

The disciple should recognize the whole movement of the chapter. The people begin in danger, facing something they cannot overcome. God calls them to turn with their whole hearts, promises mercy and restoration, and then reveals a future in which His Spirit will be poured out upon His people. The final invitation is to call upon the name of the Lord.

Joel began by sounding the alarm. Now the disciple can see why the warning was given. The Lord is calling His people back to Himself, and the promise before them is greater than the harvest they have lost.

## The Savior Test

Joel gives the people a direct command:

> "Turn ye even to me with all your heart." — Joel 2:12

Jesus begins his public preaching with the same essential call:

> "Repent: for the kingdom of heaven is at hand." — Matthew 4:17

The disciple should recognize that repentance is central to both messages. Joel calls Judah to return to the Lord, and Jesus calls men to repent because the kingdom of heaven is at hand. The invitation requires a real change of direction. The man who has been walking according to his own desires must become willing to receive the rule of God.

Joel then says:

> "And rend your heart, and not your garments." — Joel 2:13

Jesus confirms the importance of what happens within the man:

> "Blessed are the pure in heart: for they shall see God." — Matthew 5:8

And when he confronts the religious leaders, Jesus tells them:

> "Thou blind Pharisee, cleanse first that which is within the cup and platter, that the outside of them may be clean also." — Matthew 23:26

This confirms Joel's warning against outward repentance that has not reached the heart. The disciple may perform the familiar acts of religion, express sorrow, or speak about changing his ways, but Jesus directs his attention to the condition within. The heart must be cleansed, and the life that follows must agree with the change he professes.

Then we come to God's promise of restoration:

> "And I will restore to you the years that the locust hath eaten." — Joel 2:25

Jesus gives us a beautiful picture of restoration when he tells the parable of the prodigal son. The younger son has wasted his inheritance and brought himself into a condition of poverty. When he returns, his father receives him with compassion and restores him to the household.

The father declares:

> "For this my son was dead, and is alive again; he was lost, and is found." — Luke 15:24

The son cannot recover the years he spent in the far country. What he has wasted is truly gone. But he can be restored to fellowship with his father, and the life before him is changed because he has come home.

The disciple should recognize the relationship between these passages. Joel promises the restoration of a devastated land, while Jesus describes the restoration of a lost son. In both, the mercy of God becomes visible after the turning. A man may not be able to undo everything he has done, but he can return to the Father and begin living from the relationship he had abandoned.

Then Joel gives the great promise of the Spirit:

> "And it shall come to pass afterward, that I will pour out my spirit upon all flesh." — Joel 2:28

Jesus speaks directly about the coming of the Holy Spirit:

> "And I will pray the Father, and he shall give you another Comforter, that he may abide with you for ever." — John 14:16

And before his ascension, Jesus tells the disciples:

> "But ye shall receive power, after that the Holy Ghost is come upon you: and ye shall be witnesses unto me both in Jerusalem, and in all Judaea, and in Samaria, and unto the uttermost part of the earth." — Acts 1:8

Here the connection becomes especially strong. Joel promised that God would pour out His Spirit. Jesus promises the Comforter and tells the disciples that they will receive power when the Holy Ghost comes upon them.

The book of Acts then gives us an explicit fulfillment. On the day of Pentecost, when the Holy Spirit is poured out and the gathered people hear the disciples speaking in other tongues, Peter stands and explains:

> "But this is that which was spoken by the prophet Joel." — Acts 2:16

Peter then quotes Joel's prophecy concerning the Spirit being poured out upon all flesh.

This is one of the clearest New Testament confirmations of an Old Testament prophecy. Peter identifies what has happened at Pentecost with the promise Joel gave centuries earlier.

The disciple should understand how important this is. Joel's promise is not merely a poetic picture of renewed enthusiasm or a metaphor for improved circumstances. The apostles understood it as the promise of the Holy Spirit, now being poured out through the work of the risen Christ.

Jesus gives another revealing promise concerning the Spirit:

> "If any man thirst, let him come unto me, and drink." — John 7:37

John explains that Jesus was speaking of the Spirit which those who believed on him would receive.

This brings the disciple back to the beginning of Joel. The land had been devastated, the streams were dried up, and the people were facing the loss of everything that sustained them. God promised rain, fruitful fields, and renewed provision. But beyond these earthly blessings stood the greater promise of His Spirit.

Jesus invites the thirsty to come to him and drink.

Finally, Joel declares:

> "And it shall come to pass, that whosoever shall call on the name of the LORD shall be delivered." — Joel 2:32

Jesus gives the disciple a closely related invitation:

> "And him that cometh to me I will in no wise cast out." — John 6:37

The disciple should see where the whole prophecy has been leading. God has called His people to return, promised to receive them in mercy, and declared that those who call upon His name shall be delivered. Jesus invites those who come to him and promises that they will not be cast out.

The Savior Test confirms the movement of Joel 2. Jesus calls men to repentance, directs them toward inward cleansing, reveals the Father's compassion toward those who return, and promises the Holy Spirit. Peter then identifies the outpouring at Pentecost as the fulfillment of Joel's prophecy.

The disciple must ask himself whether he has genuinely returned to God or has merely become weary of the consequences of his former way. Has he allowed the Lord to reach his heart, or is he still relying upon outward appearances of repentance? And is he willing to receive the life of the Spirit rather than continue depending entirely upon his own strength?

Joel begins with the trumpet sounding in Zion. The people are warned of judgment and called to return.

But the Lord's promise reaches far beyond the disaster. He will restore the years the locusts have eaten, and He will pour out His Spirit upon all flesh.

The disciple is being invited to return to the Father and receive the life that Christ has come to give.
