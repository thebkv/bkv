# Proverbs 13 — The Life You Are Feeding

*Minimum Sufficient Role (MSR) | BKV*

## The Chapter's Role

Proverbs 13 shows the disciple how his inner life becomes his outward experience.

The chapter moves through speech, desire, correction, work, companionship, and the consequences that follow them. These may seem like separate pieces of advice, but they are all concerned with the same thing: **what a man allows to grow within himself.**

The wise man and the fool are living under the same law. Both are continually producing something from what they accept, say, desire, and practice.

The difference is that one is willing to be taught, while the other keeps defending what he already thinks.

## The Inner Movement

The chapter begins with a son receiving his father's instruction.

> A wise son heareth his father's instruction: but a scorner heareth not rebuke. (13:1)

This is the first condition of growth. The disciple must become teachable.

Correction is especially important because a man can become attached to the very thinking that keeps him in trouble. He may defend his resentment, justify his speech, or continue pursuing something that has repeatedly disappointed him.

The scorner refuses correction because he believes he already sees clearly.

The wise son listens.

The chapter then turns to the mouth:

> A man shall eat good by the fruit of his mouth: but the soul of the transgressors shall eat violence. (13:2)

> He that keepeth his mouth keepeth his life: but he that openeth wide his lips shall have destruction. (13:3)

Speech reveals what a man has been cultivating inwardly. His words also strengthen those thoughts through repetition.

The disciple therefore learns to watch what he says, especially when angry, afraid, or disappointed. He begins to recognize how readily he can give life to a thought simply by continuing to speak from it.

Then comes desire.

> The soul of the sluggard desireth, and hath nothing: but the soul of the diligent shall be made fat. (13:4)

The sluggard has desires. He may have very strong desires. What he lacks is the willingness to bring his life into agreement with them.

The diligent man participates in what he seeks.

This distinction returns later:

> Hope deferred maketh the heart sick: but when the desire cometh, it is a tree of life. (13:12)

A desire continually postponed can become a source of suffering. The disciple may spend years imagining a different life while continuing to think and act in the old way.

But the fulfillment of a rightly cultivated desire becomes a tree of life. It bears fruit beyond the original longing.

The chapter also warns about companionship:

> He that walketh with wise men shall be wise: but a companion of fools shall be destroyed. (13:20)

We become accustomed to the thinking we keep company with.

This includes the company we keep inwardly. A man can repeatedly entertain foolish arguments, old injuries, and fearful expectations until they begin to seem reasonable to him.

He must learn to recognize what kind of company his thoughts are keeping.

## The Governing Principle

The chapter states its central law plainly:

> The desire accomplished is sweet to the soul: but it is abomination to fools to depart from evil. (13:19)

Here is the difficulty.

A man wants the sweetness of fulfillment, but he may be unwilling to depart from the thinking and behavior that prevent it.

He wants peace while continuing to rehearse conflict. He wants wisdom while resisting instruction. He wants a different harvest while continuing to sow the same seed.

Proverbs 13 repeatedly brings him back to this choice.

## What Changes in the Disciple

The disciple begins to take responsibility for what he is cultivating.

He becomes more willing to receive correction. He pays attention to his words. He recognizes the difference between wanting something and faithfully working toward it.

He also learns patience.

> Wealth gotten by vanity shall be diminished: but he that gathereth by labour shall increase. (13:11)

The chapter favors steady cultivation over the attempt to obtain something without undergoing the growth that accompanies it.

And when he discovers something wrong in himself, he becomes willing to correct it rather than protect it.

## The Savior Test

Jesus gives the same principle in the Sermon on the Mount:

> Ye shall know them by their fruits. Do men gather grapes of thorns, or figs of thistles? (Matthew 7:16)

And again:

> A good man out of the good treasure of his heart bringeth forth that which is good; and an evil man out of the evil treasure of his heart bringeth forth that which is evil: for of the abundance of the heart his mouth speaketh. (Luke 6:45)

Jesus locates the source of the fruit within the man.

That confirms the inward reading of Proverbs 13. The disciple examines what he is storing in his heart because he knows it will eventually appear in his words, conduct, and experience.

## The Minimum Sufficient Role

**Proverbs 13 teaches the disciple to recognize that he is continually cultivating the life he experiences through what he accepts, speaks, desires, and practices. Wisdom begins when he becomes willing to receive correction and change what he has been cultivating.**

The chapter's essential movement is from wanting a better life to becoming a man capable of producing better fruit.
