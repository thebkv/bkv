# ISAIAH 1

## THE GREAT SUMMONS

*The Faithful City Is Called Back to God*

---

## BKV Insight

**Isaiah opens with a summons. Heaven and earth are called to hear because God's own children have risen against Him.**

The chapter begins on the scale of creation:

> "Hear, O heavens, and give ear, O earth: for the LORD hath spoken."

Then Isaiah brings us directly into the condition of the people.

An ox knows its owner. A donkey knows its master's feeding place. Israel has lost this basic recognition. The nation is sick from head to foot. The land is desolate. Jerusalem remains standing like a small shelter left behind in an emptied field.

Yet the temple worship continues.

Sacrifices are offered. Incense burns. Sabbaths and appointed gatherings are observed. Hands are lifted in prayer.

Isaiah looks at those same hands and sees blood.

God then calls the people to wash, become clean, learn to do well, seek judgment, relieve the oppressed, defend the fatherless, and plead for the widow.

Then comes the invitation:

> "Come now, and let us reason together, saith the LORD."

Scarlet can become white as snow.

Crimson can become like wool.

The chapter finally turns toward **Jerusalem**, the city that once carried faithfulness and justice. Isaiah sees what it has become, and he also sees what God intends to do with it.

Its silver will be purified.

Its judges will be restored.

Its counselors will return.

And afterward Jerusalem will again be called:

> "The city of righteousness, the faithful city."

Isaiah begins his book with the whole pattern already present.

God calls.

The true condition is revealed.

The people are invited to return.

The corrupted city passes through purification.

The faithful city appears again.

---

## Canonical History & Context

Isaiah identifies the historical world of his prophecy immediately:

> "The vision of Isaiah the son of Amoz, which he saw concerning Judah and Jerusalem in the days of Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah, kings of Judah."

These four kings place Isaiah across a long and turbulent period of Judah's history.

**Uzziah** presided over a period of considerable strength and prosperity, though his reign ended under the shadow of his unlawful entrance into the temple and his resulting leprosy.

**Jotham** inherited a relatively strong kingdom.

Under **Ahaz**, Judah faced the Syro-Ephraimite crisis. Ahaz responded to the threat by turning toward Assyria.

During **Hezekiah's** reign, Assyria itself came against Judah, capturing fortified cities and eventually threatening Jerusalem.

Isaiah therefore speaks through years of prosperity, political fear, invasion, religious corruption, reform, and deliverance.

His stated subject is:

**Judah and Jerusalem.**

That distinction will matter throughout the book.

Judah is the kingdom.

Jerusalem is its central city, the location of the temple and Davidic throne.

In Isaiah, Jerusalem can therefore function at several levels at once: an actual city, the covenant center of Judah, the location of God's sanctuary, and eventually the restored city toward which the prophetic vision moves.

Isaiah 1 introduces all of this before the rest of the book unfolds.

# Natural Reading

## Isaiah 1:1–3 — Heaven and Earth Are Called to Hear

### What Happens

Isaiah identifies himself, his subject, and the kings during whose reigns he received his vision.

Then God speaks:

> "Hear, O heavens, and give ear, O earth."

Heaven and earth become witnesses.

God says:

> "I have nourished and brought up children, and they have rebelled against me."

Then Isaiah gives us two ordinary animals:

**an ox and a donkey.**

> "The ox knoweth his owner, and the ass his master's crib: but Israel doth not know, my people doth not consider."

The animals recognize the one who owns and feeds them.

Israel has stopped recognizing God.

### The Key

The opening summons reaches back to the covenant language of Moses.

In Deuteronomy 30–32, heaven and earth are called as witnesses concerning Israel's covenant with God. Moses begins his song:

> "Give ear, O ye heavens, and I will speak; and hear, O earth, the words of my mouth."  
> — Deuteronomy 32:1

Isaiah opens in the same courtroom.

Heaven and earth have been summoned again.

The charge is rebellion.

And Isaiah makes the problem painfully simple with the ox and donkey.

These animals possess enough recognition to know where they belong and where their provision comes from.

Israel has lost even that.

### BKV

Isaiah begins with recognition.

Before the chapter reaches sacrifice, morality, government, or judgment, it asks a basic question:

**Do you know whose you are?**

The ox knows its owner.

The donkey knows the place from which it is fed.

The disciple's return begins with the same recognition.

God is present.

Life comes from Him.

We belong to Him.

Isaiah's entire prophetic journey begins there.

---

## Isaiah 1:4–9 — The Whole Body Is Sick

### What Happens

Isaiah now describes the condition of the nation:

> "Ah sinful nation, a people laden with iniquity..."

They have forsaken the LORD and gone backward.

Then the nation becomes a wounded body:

> "The whole head is sick, and the whole heart faint."

From the sole of the foot to the head there are wounds, bruises, and open sores.

Then the image expands again.

The body becomes a land.

Cities are burned.

Foreigners consume the fields.

The **daughter of Zion** remains like a small hut in a vineyard or a lodge in a garden of cucumbers.

Almost everything around her has been stripped away.

Isaiah says that if the LORD had not left a small remnant, they would have become like **Sodom and Gomorrah**.

### The Key

Isaiah moves easily between body, land, city, and people because they are different ways of describing the condition of the same covenant community.

The wounds have not been treated.

The land has been overrun.

Yet Zion remains.

That small surviving presence matters.

Isaiah will return to the remnant repeatedly throughout the book.

Something survives the devastation.

### BKV

Isaiah's wounded body gives us a remarkably direct interior picture.

The **head** is sick.

The **heart** is faint.

The condition reaches all the way to the feet.

Thought, desire, direction, and action are all affected.

Isaiah then shows the same condition as damaged territory.

The interior kingdom has lost ground.

Yet something remains.

Zion is still there.

It may look like a little shelter standing alone after the field has been stripped, but the place associated with God's dwelling has survived.

The restoration will begin from what remains.

---

## Isaiah 1:10–15 — Worship Continues

### What Happens

Isaiah suddenly addresses the rulers and people as **Sodom and Gomorrah**.

Then God speaks about their worship.

They are still bringing sacrifices.

They still appear in the courts.

They still burn incense.

They still observe new moons, Sabbaths, and assemblies.

They still spread out their hands in prayer.

God says:

> "I am full of the burnt offerings of rams..."

And:

> "When ye spread forth your hands, I will hide mine eyes from you."

Then Isaiah tells us why:

> "Your hands are full of blood."

### The Key

The worship has continued while the life represented by that worship has become corrupt.

This becomes especially clear in the verses that follow.

God immediately tells them what He wants to see:

**wash, become clean, stop doing evil, learn to do well, seek judgment, relieve the oppressed, defend the fatherless, plead for the widow.**

Isaiah is showing a separation between the sanctuary and the life.

The rituals are still happening.

The covenant order they express is no longer governing the people.

### BKV

A person can continue religious activity while the interior government remains unchanged.

Words can be spoken.

Prayers can be prayed.

Scripture can be read.

Hands can be raised.

Isaiah looks at the hands.

The question becomes very concrete:

**What are these same hands doing when the prayer is finished?**

The disciple is being called toward an integrated life, where worship and action belong to the same kingdom.

---

## Isaiah 1:16–20 — Wash and Learn

### What Happens

God gives the people a series of commands:

> "Wash you, make you clean."

Then:

> "Learn to do well."

Isaiah becomes very practical.

Seek judgment.

Relieve the oppressed.

Judge the fatherless.

Plead for the widow.

Then God says:

> "Come now, and let us reason together."

The scarlet condition can become white as snow.

The crimson condition can become like wool.

The people are then presented with a real choice:

> "If ye be willing and obedient, ye shall eat the good of the land."

And:

> "If ye refuse and rebel, ye shall be devoured with the sword."

### The Key

Isaiah's call to cleansing is followed immediately by changed action.

The chapter itself tells us what becoming clean looks like.

Justice returns.

The vulnerable are defended.

The oppressed receive help.

The invitation to "reason together" comes in the middle of this call to return.

God is summoning His people back into agreement with Him.

### BKV

The movement has become personal.

**Wash.  
Learn.  
Seek.  
Relieve.  
Defend.  
Plead.  
Come.**

The disciple is being called back into alignment with the character of God.

Then comes one of the great invitations in Scripture:

> "Come now, and let us reason together."

God addresses a people covered in scarlet and speaks of snow.

The present condition is real.

So is the possibility of cleansing.

The summons is still open.

---

## Isaiah 1:21–23 — What Happened to the Faithful City?

### What Happens

Isaiah turns toward Jerusalem:

> "How is the faithful city become an harlot!"

Then he remembers what the city had been:

> "It was full of judgment; righteousness lodged in it."

Something has happened to Jerusalem.

Isaiah describes it with two images:

> "Thy silver is become dross, thy wine mixed with water."

The rulers are rebellious.

They associate with thieves.

They love gifts and pursue rewards.

The fatherless and widow receive no justice from them.

### The Key

Jerusalem is especially important because Isaiah tells us both its former condition and its present condition.

It was the **faithful city**.

Righteousness once lodged there.

Now the silver contains dross.

The wine has been diluted.

The rulers who should administer justice have become part of the corruption.

The city still exists.

Its governing quality has changed.

### BKV

Jerusalem becomes one of the great interior locations of Scripture.

It is the city of the King.

It contains the sanctuary.

It is associated with worship, government, judgment, and peace.

Isaiah looks at that city and finds mixture.

The silver is still silver, but dross is mixed through it.

The wine is still present, but it has been watered down.

This is a condition the disciple can recognize.

Something true remains, yet it is mixed with motives, fears, habits, and loyalties that do not belong with it.

Isaiah is about to tell us what God does with mixed silver.

---

## Isaiah 1:24–27 — The Silver Is Refined

### What Happens

God declares:

> "I will turn my hand upon thee, and purely purge away thy dross, and take away all thy tin."

Then He speaks of Jerusalem's government:

> "And I will restore thy judges as at the first, and thy counsellors as at the beginning."

Afterward the city will receive its old names again:

> "The city of righteousness, the faithful city."

Then Isaiah says:

> "Zion shall be redeemed with judgment, and her converts with righteousness."

### The Key

This is the destination of the chapter.

Isaiah introduced the corrupted silver in verse 22.

Now God purifies it.

The same hand that judges is the hand performing the refining work.

Then the judges and counselors are restored.

The restoration of the city includes the restoration of its government.

And when that work is complete, its true name fits again:

**the faithful city.**

### BKV

The dross is removed from the silver.

That is a different picture from throwing the silver away.

Something valuable has been mixed with something that does not belong in it.

The refining separates them.

Isaiah's vision of judgment therefore reaches toward restoration.

The interior Jerusalem is being brought back into order.

Judgment returns.

Counsel returns.

Righteousness lives there again.

The city becomes faithful because the life within it has come back under the government of God.

---

## Isaiah 1:28–31 — The False Desire Burns Out

### What Happens

Isaiah closes with the fate of those who continue in rebellion.

Then he mentions the **oaks** and **gardens** the people had desired and chosen.

These appear to be places connected with idolatrous worship.

The people will become ashamed of them.

Isaiah says:

> "Ye shall be as an oak whose leaf fadeth, and as a garden that hath no water."

Then:

> "The strong shall be as tow, and the maker of it as a spark, and they shall both burn together, and none shall quench them."

### The Key

The chapter ends with chosen sources of life failing.

The desired oak loses its leaves.

The chosen garden loses its water.

The thing trusted for strength becomes dry enough to burn.

Isaiah has returned to the question with which he began:

**Where does life come from?**

The ox knew its owner.

The donkey knew its master's crib.

Israel had forgotten.

The dry garden shows where that forgetting eventually leads.

### BKV

A garden lives by its source.

Cut off from water, its appearance cannot be sustained.

Isaiah has spent the chapter calling the disciple back to the true source.

Recognition comes first.

Then cleansing.

Then restored judgment and counsel.

Then the city becomes faithful again.

The summons that began in heaven and earth has reached all the way into Jerusalem.

# Fractal Notes & References

## Heaven and Earth Are Witnesses

Isaiah's first words deliberately recall Moses:

> "Give ear, O ye heavens, and I will speak; and hear, O earth, the words of my mouth."  
> — Deuteronomy 32:1

Isaiah:

> "Hear, O heavens, and give ear, O earth."

Isaiah is continuing the covenant story.

The people Moses warned have now reached the condition his song anticipated.

Isaiah's prophecy begins as another great covenant summons.

---

## The Ox and the Donkey

The first spiritual failure Isaiah identifies is remarkably basic:

**Israel does not know.**

The ox recognizes its owner.

The donkey recognizes the place where it is fed.

Isaiah therefore places recognition beneath everything that follows.

Before the faithful city can be restored, the people must remember the One to whom they belong.

---

## Body → Land → City

Isaiah moves through three scales.

The nation is a **body** covered in wounds.

Then it is a **land** overrun by strangers.

Then attention narrows toward **Zion and Jerusalem**.

The same disorder is being shown at different scales.

This is one of Isaiah's clearest early fractal movements.

The condition of the whole nation appears again inside its governing city.

---

## Sodom and Gomorrah

Isaiah uses these names twice.

First, he says Judah would have become like Sodom and Gomorrah if God had not preserved a remnant.

Then he directly calls Judah's rulers "rulers of Sodom" and the people "people of Gomorrah."

Their minimum role is supplied by Scripture itself: they are the remembered pattern of a society whose corruption has ripened toward destruction.

Isaiah uses that old story as a mirror for Jerusalem's present condition.

---

## The Hands

Isaiah gives us a simple physical connection:

> "When ye spread forth your hands..."

Then:

> "Your hands are full of blood."

The same hands appear in worship and in ordinary life.

Isaiah refuses to separate them.

The body that enters the sanctuary is the body that leaves it.

---

## Scarlet and Snow

Isaiah moves from one visible condition to another:

**scarlet → snow**

**crimson → wool**

The language makes cleansing something the reader can see.

The chapter has already described wounds and blood. Now the stained condition becomes clean.

The restoration is presented as a real transformation.

---

## Silver and Dross

Jerusalem's silver has become mixed with dross.

God says:

> "I will... purely purge away thy dross."

This becomes one of the governing images of the chapter.

The refining fire reveals what belongs to the silver and what does not.

The result is restoration:

**judges return → counselors return → righteousness returns → the faithful city returns.**

---

## The City Receives Its Name Again

Verse 21 remembers:

> "the faithful city"

Verse 26 promises:

> "thou shalt be called, The city of righteousness, the faithful city."

The name appears on both sides of the purification.

Isaiah remembers what Jerusalem was, walks through what it has become, and sees what it will be called after refinement.

The restored name marks restored identity.

# The Pattern Revealed

Isaiah begins by calling **heaven and earth**.

God has something to say about His children.

They have forgotten Him.

Isaiah shows that condition first through an ox and a donkey, then through a wounded body, then through a devastated land, and finally through the city of Jerusalem itself.

The worship is still happening.

Sacrifices rise from the altar.

Incense burns.

Hands stretch toward heaven.

Then Isaiah looks at the hands and sees what the life has become.

God calls the people to wash.

To learn.

To seek judgment.

To defend the vulnerable.

To come and reason with Him.

Then Isaiah enters the city.

Jerusalem was once full of judgment and righteousness. Now its silver contains dross and its wine has been diluted.

God puts His hand upon the city.

The silver is refined.

The judges return.

The counselors return.

And the city receives its name again:

**The city of righteousness.  
The faithful city.**

This is the great summons of Isaiah 1.

It begins across heaven and earth and ends inside Jerusalem.

The disciple hears the same call.

Remember whose you are.

Return to the source from which you are fed.

Let the wounds be seen.

Wash.

Learn the ways of the kingdom.

Let God separate the silver from the dross.

As the government within is restored, the city becomes what it was created to be.

**Faithful again.**
