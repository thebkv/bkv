# Exodus 2 — The Deliverer Is Drawn Out and Prepared

## BKV Insight

Exodus 2 begins with Pharaoh still ruling Egypt and Israel still in bondage. Yet within that bondage, the child through whom God will bring deliverance has already been born.

Moses first lives the pattern he will later lead Israel through. Pharaoh has commanded the Hebrew sons to be cast into the river, but Moses passes through those waters alive. He is **drawn out**, raised within Pharaoh's own house, awakens to the suffering of his brethren, leaves Egypt, becomes a stranger in Midian, and is prepared for a calling he has not yet received.

For the disciple, this gives us a concrete picture of how deliverance begins. **Something can be born within a life still under bondage that no longer truly belongs to that bondage.** At first it may be small, hidden, and surrounded by the old order. Yet God can preserve it until it is ready to become the means through which the rest of the life is brought out.

Moses cannot free Israel simply because he recognizes the oppression. His first attempt ends in bloodshed and exile. He must be drawn out of Egypt not only physically but from the authority, identity, and methods Egypt has given him.

**The deliverer must first undergo the deliverance.**

## Canonical History & Context

Exodus 2 continues directly from Pharaoh's command at the end of Exodus 1:

> “Every son that is born ye shall cast into the river.”

A man and woman of the house of Levi have a son. His mother hides him for three months and then places him in a small waterproofed **ark** among the reeds of the Nile. Pharaoh's daughter discovers him, has compassion on him, and eventually adopts him. Through the intervention of the child's sister, his own mother is brought in to nurse him.

Pharaoh's daughter names the child **Moses** because she drew him out of the water.

When Moses is grown, he goes out to his Hebrew brethren and sees their burdens. He kills an Egyptian who is beating a Hebrew. The next day, while trying to intervene between two Hebrews, Moses is challenged:

> “Who made thee a prince and a judge over us?”

When Pharaoh hears of the killing, Moses flees Egypt and settles in Midian. There he defends the daughters of the priest of Midian at a well, marries Zipporah, and names their son Gershom because he has become “a stranger in a strange land.”

The chapter closes by returning to Israel. The people groan under bondage and cry to God. God hears them, remembers His covenant, looks upon them, and knows their condition.

Israel does not yet know what the reader knows:

**the deliverer has already been born.**

## Natural Reading

### v1–2 — The Deliverer Is Born Within the Enslaved Household

> “And there went a man of the house of Levi, and took to wife a daughter of Levi.”

**Levi** is a name Genesis explicitly connects with **joining**:

> “Now this time will my husband be joined unto me.” — Genesis 29:34

From this house a son is born.

His mother sees:

> **“that he was a goodly child.”**

The text does not yet tell us what Moses will become. His mother simply sees the child, recognizes something precious in him, and protects him from Pharaoh's command.

BKV can see a larger pattern because we know where the story goes. **Within the very household that has been put in bondage, the one through whom deliverance will come has now been born.**

For the disciple, the beginning of deliverance may appear while much of the life is still serving the old ruler. A new recognition of God, a desire for truth, a refusal of some old pattern, or the first movement toward obedience may be very small at first.

It does not yet rule the life.

But it is alive.

### v3–4 — The Ark Enters the River

When Moses' mother can no longer hide him, she makes:

> **“an ark of bulrushes”**

and seals it with slime and pitch.

Then she places the child among the reeds by the river.

This is a remarkable reversal of Pharaoh's command. Pharaoh ordered the Hebrew sons to be thrown into the river to die. Moses enters the same river **inside an ark that preserves him**.

The Hebrew word used here, **tevah**, is the same unusual word used for Noah's ark in Genesis. These are its only two biblical settings.

In Genesis, an ark carries Noah through waters of destruction.

In Exodus, an ark carries Moses through waters Pharaoh intended for destruction.

The water remains dangerous. **What changes is that God has provided a means of preservation through it.**

His sister stands at a distance to see what will happen.

### v5–10 — Moses Is Drawn Out

Pharaoh's daughter comes to the river and sees the ark.

She opens it.

The child is crying.

> **“And she had compassion on him.”**

She recognizes that he is:

> “one of the Hebrews' children.”

She knows what kind of child he is, yet she preserves him.

The house of Pharaoh therefore becomes involved in protecting the very Hebrew son Pharaoh's command was meant to destroy.

Moses' sister then offers to find a Hebrew nurse. His own mother is called and is even paid to nurse the child.

So Moses will eventually be raised as the son of Pharaoh's daughter, but his earliest nourishment still comes from his Hebrew mother.

When the child grows, he is brought to Pharaoh's daughter:

> “and he became her son.”

Then comes the name that gives the chapter one of its clearest keys:

> **“And she called his name Moses: and she said, Because I drew him out of the water.”**

**Moses — Drawn Out.**

Before Moses draws Israel out of Egypt, Moses himself is drawn out.

Before Israel passes safely through the waters, Moses passes safely through the waters.

Before Pharaoh loses Israel, Pharaoh's own household unknowingly raises the man through whom Israel will be delivered.

The Exodus has begun in miniature.

For the disciple, the pattern is powerful. **The part of the life through which God will eventually challenge bondage may first have to be preserved while surrounded by that bondage.** It can even grow for a time inside structures belonging to the old life without finally belonging to them.

Moses grows in Pharaoh's house.

But Moses is not Pharaoh's.

### v11–12 — Moses Sees the Burdens

When Moses is grown:

> **“he went out unto his brethren, and looked on their burdens.”**

The wording is important.

The Hebrews are his **brethren**.

Moses has lived in Pharaoh's house, but he now identifies himself with the people Pharaoh has enslaved.

He sees an Egyptian beating a Hebrew.

Moses looks around, sees no one, kills the Egyptian, and hides him in the sand.

His concern for the oppressed is real.

His method is not yet the method by which God will deliver Israel.

Moses sees the bondage and acts immediately through his own strength. The man who will eventually confront Pharaoh under God's command has not yet received that command.

For the disciple, recognizing bondage is not the same thing as being prepared to overcome it. **We can correctly see what is wrong and still try to correct it through the same self-directed power that belongs to the old way of life.**

Moses will indeed act with tremendous authority.

But first that authority must come from God.

### v13–14 — “Who Made Thee a Prince and a Judge?”

The next day Moses sees two Hebrews fighting.

He asks the one doing wrong:

> “Wherefore smitest thou thy fellow?”

The man answers:

> **“Who made thee a prince and a judge over us?”**

The question reaches directly into Moses' condition.

Who made you?

Moses will later exercise authority among Israel. He will judge disputes. He will stand before Pharaoh. He will lead the people out.

But none of that authority has yet been given to him.

He has seen the suffering.

He wants to intervene.

**He has not yet been sent.**

For the disciple, there is a difference between recognizing a calling and possessing authority from God to carry it out. The natural governing mind can seize even a true spiritual purpose and attempt to accomplish it by its own force.

Moses has to learn something deeper than how to defeat an Egyptian.

He has to learn how God delivers.

### v15 — Moses Leaves Egypt

When Pharaoh hears what Moses has done:

> **“he sought to slay Moses.”**

Moses flees.

The man who was drawn out of the Nile is now drawn out of Pharaoh's world.

He comes to **Midian** and sits by a well.

The text gives us the physical movement first:

**Pharaoh's house → wilderness → Midian.**

BKV sees the corresponding inner movement. The deliverer cannot remain identified with the order he will later confront. Moses loses his place in Pharaoh's house before he receives his place in God's work.

He is no longer living as an Egyptian prince.

He is not yet leading Israel.

He is between the two.

### v16–17 — At the Well, Moses Delivers Without Killing

The priest of Midian has seven daughters. They come to the well:

> “and drew water, and filled the troughs to water their father's flock.”

Shepherds come and drive them away.

Then:

> **“Moses stood up and helped them, and watered their flock.”**

Once again Moses sees stronger people mistreating others.

Once again he intervenes.

But the story now gives us a different outcome.

In Egypt, Moses sees oppression and **kills**.

At the well, Moses sees oppression and **helps**.

Then he waters the flock.

The text does not tell us that this single event completes Moses' preparation, but the contrast is worth seeing. His action now preserves and serves rather than destroys.

Water also appears again.

At the Nile, Moses was **drawn from water**.

At the well, Moses helps **draw water for others**.

The man who was preserved is already becoming a man who preserves.

### v18–20 — The Egyptian Who Acts Like a Deliverer

The daughters return to their father, **Reuel**, earlier than expected.

They explain:

> **“An Egyptian delivered us out of the hand of the shepherds, and also drew water enough for us, and watered the flock.”**

They call Moses:

> **“an Egyptian.”**

That is what he looks like to them.

Yet look at what this “Egyptian” does:

> **“delivered us”**

and:

> **“drew water enough for us.”**

His outward identity still appears Egyptian, but his actions already point toward his coming work.

He delivers.

He draws.

He waters.

Reuel tells his daughters to bring Moses into the house:

> “call him, that he may eat bread.”

Moses has left Pharaoh's household and is received into another household.

### v21–22 — Gershom: A Stranger There

Moses remains with the man and marries his daughter **Zipporah**.

They have a son.

Moses names him:

> **“Gershom: for he said, I have been a stranger in a strange land.”**

As with Moses' own name, Scripture tells us what Moses intends the name to mean.

**Moses — Drawn Out.**

**Gershom — A Stranger There.**

The names trace Moses' movement.

He has been drawn out.

Now he knows himself as a stranger.

Egypt gave him a home, education, position, and identity, but he can no longer simply belong to Egypt. At the same time, he has not yet returned to Israel as its deliverer.

For the disciple, there can be a similar interval in spiritual life. **The old identity no longer fits, while the new work God is preparing has not yet fully appeared.**

That interval can feel like exile.

For Moses, it becomes preparation.

**Drawn Out  
→ Stranger  
→ Prepared to Return**

### v23–25 — Israel Cries, and God Knows

The chapter now leaves Moses in Midian and returns to Egypt.

The king of Egypt dies.

But Israel's bondage does not die with him.

> **“And the children of Israel sighed by reason of the bondage, and they cried.”**

Their cry rises to God.

Then the text gives us a remarkable sequence:

> **“God heard their groaning”**

> **“God remembered his covenant”**

> **“God looked upon the children of Israel”**

> **“God had respect unto them.”**

The final Hebrew expression carries the sense that **God knew**.

Israel has now become painfully aware of its bondage, and its cry is directed upward.

For the disciple, this is another beginning of deliverance: **the condition that has been endured becomes something we finally cry to God about.** We stop treating bondage as though it were simply the way life must be.

Yet the reader knows something Israel does not.

While the people are crying in Egypt, Moses is already alive in Midian.

While Israel sees only bondage, the deliverer has already been born, preserved, drawn out, separated, and prepared for the encounter with God that will come next.

**The cry and God's answer are already moving toward one another.**

## Fractal Notes & References

Exodus 2 develops a pattern that appears repeatedly in Scripture:

**the deliverer appears while the old ruler still seems secure  
→ the deliverer is threatened  
→ God preserves him  
→ he passes through separation and preparation  
→ he returns under divine authority.**

The connection between Noah and Moses is especially strong because Scripture uses **tevah — ark** only for their two vessels. Noah is preserved through the floodwaters. Moses is preserved through the waters Pharaoh had appointed for death.

Moses also begins to embody Israel's coming Exodus.

**Moses passes through the waters.  
Israel will pass through the waters.**

**Moses leaves Pharaoh's house.  
Israel will leave Pharaoh's land.**

**Moses becomes a stranger outside Egypt.  
Israel will become a people in the wilderness.**

**Moses must meet God before returning to Pharaoh.  
Israel is being brought out so that it may meet and serve God.**

The threatened infancy of Moses also has a strong canonical echo in Matthew 2. Pharaoh seeks the death of Hebrew sons while Moses is preserved; Herod later orders the killing of male children around Bethlehem while Jesus is preserved. In both stories, the ruler who appears politically powerful cannot prevent God's appointed deliverer from surviving.

For BKV, Moses gives us a particularly useful picture of the emerging power of deliverance within the disciple. The rest of the faculties may still be working under Pharaoh, yet **the power through which God will challenge that government can already be growing within the same life.**

It should not be confused with the natural governing mind simply deciding to improve itself. Moses' failed act in Egypt guards against that reading. He recognizes the bondage before he has learned how God will free the people.

The movement is therefore:

**recognition  
→ self-directed action  
→ separation  
→ preparation  
→ divine commission.**

Exodus 3 will supply what Exodus 2 deliberately withholds:

**God will speak.**

## Onomastics & Key Terms

| Name / Term | Textual / Linguistic Meaning | Minimum Functional Role / BKV Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **Levi — Levi** | Genesis 29:34 explicitly connects the name with **joining / being joined** | The tribal household from which Moses is born. The association with joining is textually strong; any further fixed faculty assignment remains a BKV correspondence |
| **Moses — Mosheh** | Exodus 2:10 explains the name through *mashah*, **“to draw out”**: “because I drew him out of the water” | **Drawn Out.** Moses first experiences the pattern he will later lead Israel through |
| **Ark — Tevah** | The unusual Hebrew word used for Noah's ark and Moses' small vessel; these are its only biblical settings | A vessel of preservation through waters associated with destruction |
| **Egypt — Mitsrayim** | Historical name of Egypt; Exodus later calls it **“the house of bondage”** | The land ruled by Pharaoh from which Moses is separated and Israel will be delivered; in BKV, the condition in which God-given powers serve a foreign ruler |
| **Pharaoh** | Egyptian royal title | The ruler whose command threatens Moses and whose government holds Israel in bondage; a strong BKV image of the foreign governing power |
| **Midian — Midyan** | Commonly associated with **strife / contention**, though the exact etymological history deserves caution | The actual land to which Moses flees. In the chapter's movement it becomes his place of separation and preparation before divine commission |
| **Reuel — Reʿuel** | Commonly understood as **Friend / companion of God** | The priest of Midian who receives Moses after his flight from Pharaoh's house; the name gives a fitting secondary resonance without needing to carry the interpretation |
| **Zipporah — Tsipporah** | **Bird** | Moses' wife in Midian. The meaning is clear, but Exodus 2 does not give enough evidence to assign “bird” a precise inner function |
| **Gershom — Gershom** | Moses explains the name: **“I have been a stranger in a strange land”** | **A Stranger There / Sojourner.** The name records Moses' separation from his former place and identity |
| **Israel — Yisrael** | Genesis 32:28 connects the name with Jacob's striving with God and prevailing | The covenant people still under bondage; in BKV, the God-given powers of the person awaiting release from foreign government |

Two names deserve special attention because the chapter itself explains them:

**Moses — Drawn Out.**

**Gershom — A Stranger There.**

Together they tell the story of the chapter:

**DRAWN OUT  
→ MADE A STRANGER  
→ PREPARED TO RETURN**

Before Moses can draw Israel out of Egypt, he must cease to belong to Pharaoh's order.

Before he can act with divine authority, he must discover that seeing bondage is not enough.

And while Israel cries without yet seeing an answer, **the man God will send has already been preserved and prepared.**
