# ROMANS 1

## 01 — THE GREAT EXCHANGE

Paul opens his letter to the Romans by introducing himself as a servant of Jesus Christ, called to be an apostle and separated unto the gospel of God. He tells them that this gospel was promised beforehand through the prophets and concerns Jesus Christ, who came of the seed of David according to the flesh and was declared to be the Son of God with power by the resurrection from the dead.

Paul is preparing the disciple to understand something much greater than a new religious teaching. The gospel announces that the power of God has entered human life through Jesus Christ. The promises made through the prophets have reached their fulfillment, and the same power that raised Jesus from the dead is now being proclaimed to the nations.

Paul longs to visit the believers in Rome so that they may be established and encouraged together in their faith. He is not ashamed of the gospel, because it is "the power of God unto salvation to every one that believeth." He then gives the declaration upon which much of the letter will unfold: "The just shall live by faith."

The disciple must begin here. Paul is not offering him another set of human methods for making himself righteous. He is directing him toward the living power of God received through faith. The disciple is learning to trust what God has accomplished through Christ and to allow that faith to govern his life.

Then Paul turns to the condition from which the gospel delivers man.

He declares that the wrath of God is revealed against ungodliness and unrighteousness, particularly against those who hold the truth in unrighteousness. He explains that something of God has already been made manifest through the things He has created. The world bears witness to its Creator, yet men have refused to honor Him.

This is the first great warning. The problem begins before the outward sins appear. Paul describes people who possess some knowledge of God but refuse to glorify Him as God or give Him thanks. Their imaginations become vain, and their foolish heart is darkened. Professing themselves wise, they become fools.

The disciple should recognize how easily this can happen within his own thinking. He may have received a genuine understanding of God, yet gradually begin to trust his own conclusions more than the truth that brought him to that understanding. Instead of giving thanks for what he has received, he begins to treat himself as the source of his wisdom. What began as knowledge becomes an occasion for pride.

Paul describes the turning point as an exchange. Men change the glory of the incorruptible God into images resembling corruptible creatures. They exchange the truth of God for a lie, and worship and serve the creature more than the Creator.

This is the great exchange at the center of the chapter. (continues)

The disciple should not imagine that an idol must always be a carved image. Anything created can become an object of misplaced devotion when a man begins to look to it for what belongs to God. His possessions, reputation, physical strength, intellectual ability, or even his religious accomplishments can become the things around which he organizes his life.

The danger appears when he begins to depend upon the gift instead of the Giver. He may continue to speak about God while allowing something else to occupy the place of greatest authority in his heart.

Paul then describes the consequences. Three times he uses the expression that God gave them up or gave them over. Having exchanged the truth for a lie, they are given over to uncleanness, dishonorable passions, and finally a reprobate mind.

There is a serious principle here. When a man persistently refuses the truth, the desires he has chosen begin to exercise greater authority over him. What once seemed like freedom can become bondage. His thinking begins to justify conduct that he might once have recognized as destructive, and his desires increasingly govern his decisions.

Paul describes sexual disorder among the consequences of this departure from God. He then gives a much wider account of unrighteousness, including covetousness, malice, envy, murder, deceit, pride, disobedience to parents, and the absence of mercy.

The disciple must not read this as though Paul were merely providing a list by which he could condemn other people. Paul is exposing the ruin that follows when human beings reject God's rightful place in their lives. The same chapter that describes outward immorality also warns against pride, boasting, deceit, and the refusal to show mercy. No disciple should suppose that he has escaped its warning simply because he does not recognize himself in one particular sin.

One of the most revealing details is that Paul connects corrupt conduct with a corrupted understanding. The mind does not remain unaffected by what a man chooses to love and serve. As he continues to embrace falsehood, he becomes increasingly capable of defending it.

The chapter ends with people who know the judgment of God, yet not only continue in unrighteousness but take pleasure in others who do the same.

Here the disciple sees how far the exchange can reach. What began as a refusal to honor God becomes a condition in which a man celebrates the very things that are destroying him.

But Paul has already told us why he is writing. The gospel is the power of God unto salvation. He has described the darkness so that the disciple may understand the greatness of the deliverance offered in Jesus Christ.

The chapter therefore asks a searching question. What has the disciple placed at the center of his life? Is he receiving the truth of God with thanksgiving and faith, or has he begun to serve something that was never meant to rule him?

The Creator has given him life. The gospel now calls him to return to the One from whom that life comes.

## The Savior Test

Jesus confirms the central warning of Romans 1 when he describes what happens to the heart that turns away from the light:

> "And this is the condemnation, that light is come into the world, and men loved darkness rather than light, because their deeds were evil." — John 3:19

Notice that Jesus identifies a choice. Light has come, but men love darkness rather than light. Paul describes the same movement when he speaks of those who hold the truth in unrighteousness. The problem is not simply a lack of information. It is the refusal to receive and honor the truth that has been made known.

Jesus continues:

> "But he that doeth truth cometh to the light, that his deeds may be made manifest, that they are wrought in God." — John 3:21

This gives the disciple the way forward. He must come to the light rather than defend what the light exposes. He must become willing to have his thoughts, desires, and actions examined in the presence of God.

Paul also describes the corruption that follows when the creature takes the place of the Creator. Jesus confirms this principle when he says:

> "No man can serve two masters... Ye cannot serve God and mammon." — Matthew 6:24

Jesus is speaking directly about the danger of allowing wealth to become a master. Romans 1 reveals the wider pattern. When a man gives ultimate devotion to something created, that thing begins to govern him. He may believe he possesses it, yet discover that it possesses his attention, his desires, and his decisions.

Jesus also shows us where the outward corruption begins:

> "For from within, out of the heart of men, proceed evil thoughts, adulteries, fornications, murders..." — Mark 7:21

This confirms the order Paul describes. The trouble reaches deeper than outward conduct. The heart has turned from the truth, and what has taken root within eventually appears in the life.

The disciple should therefore resist the temptation to read Romans 1 merely as an accusation against the world around him. Jesus directs his attention to the condition of his own heart. What does he love? What does he serve? What truth has he received but resisted putting into practice?

And there is a promise of freedom in the very words of Jesus:

> "If the Son therefore shall make you free, ye shall be free indeed." — John 8:36

Paul describes the bondage that follows the great exchange. Jesus announces the freedom that comes through the Son.

The disciple must examine what he has allowed to take God's place. He may have trusted wealth, admired his own understanding, pursued the approval of others, or surrendered himself to desires that now govern him. But he is not required to remain in that condition. The gospel Paul proclaims is the power of God unto salvation.

Romans 1 begins with the righteousness of God revealed through faith, and then shows the darkness that follows when men exchange His truth for a lie.

Jesus confirms both sides of that lesson. The man who loves darkness moves away from the light, but the man who does truth comes to the light.

The disciple must decide which way he is walking.
