# Exodus 15 — The Delivered Faculties Learn to Trust the LORD

## BKV Insight

Exodus 15 begins with Israel finally seeing the LORD as its strength, salvation, and King, then immediately carries that confession into the first hard test of freedom. The people sing because God has destroyed Pharaoh's power, yet three days into the wilderness bitter water is enough to bring murmuring back to the surface. The LORD heals the water, identifies Himself as **“the LORD that healeth thee,”** and leads Israel onward to abundant water at Elim. For the disciple, this is the first great lesson after separation from the old ruler: **the God who delivered you from bondage must now become the One you trust for the actual conditions of the journey.**

## Canonical History & Context

Exodus 15 follows immediately after the crossing of the Red Sea. Moses and the children of Israel sing of the LORD's victory over Pharaoh, celebrating Him as their strength, salvation, warrior, guide, and everlasting King. The song also looks ahead to the land, the mountain of God's inheritance, and the sanctuary He will establish. Miriam then leads the women in answering praise. Moses brings Israel from the Red Sea into the wilderness of Shur, where after three days without water they reach Marah and find its waters bitter. The people murmur, Moses cries to the LORD, and the LORD shows him a tree that makes the waters sweet. There God gives Israel a statute and an ordinance, tests them, and declares Himself their healer. The chapter closes at Elim, with twelve wells of water and seventy palm trees, preparing for the continuing wilderness formation of Exodus 16.

## Natural Reading

### v1–5 — The LORD Has Become My Salvation

Then sang Moses and the children of Israel unto the LORD:

> “I will sing unto the LORD, for he hath triumphed gloriously: the horse and his rider hath he thrown into the sea.”

The people who cried beneath Pharaoh now sing to the LORD who broke Pharaoh's power.

> **“The LORD is my strength and song, and he is become my salvation: he is my God.”**

Israel has seen where its strength actually lies. Pharaoh had chariots, captains, horses, armies, and the machinery of Egypt. The LORD brought them down into the sea.

The delivered faculties can now say from experience: **the LORD is my strength, and the LORD is my salvation.**

### v6–12 — Pharaoh's “I Will” Meets the Hand of God

The song turns again to the destruction of Egypt:

> “Thy right hand, O LORD, is become glorious in power.”

The enemy had said:

> “I will pursue, I will overtake, I will divide the spoil.”

Pharaoh's governing will still imagined itself able to pursue, possess, and divide Israel according to its own desire.

Then:

> “Thou didst blow with thy wind, the sea covered them.”

The LORD's action ends Egypt's claim.

Israel therefore asks:

> **“Who is like unto thee, O LORD, among the gods?”**

Pharaoh once asked, **“Who is the LORD?”** The delivered people can now answer from what they have seen. The LORD has demonstrated His authority in Egypt and at the sea, and the powers that held Israel cannot stand before Him.

### v13–18 — Redemption Moves Toward God's Dwelling

The song now looks forward:

> “Thou in thy mercy hast led forth the people which thou hast redeemed.”

And:

> “thou hast guided them in thy strength unto thy holy habitation.”

The people have crossed the sea, yet the song already sees the destination of the Exodus.

The LORD will bring them in:

> “and plant them in the mountain of thine inheritance.”

And ultimately:

> “in the Sanctuary, O Lord, which thy hands have established.”

The movement is toward **God's dwelling and God's government**.

The chapter therefore reaches its great declaration:

> **“The LORD shall reign for ever and ever.”**

Pharaoh's rule over Israel has ended. The faculties are being brought under the reign of the LORD, and the journey will eventually produce a sanctuary where His Presence dwells among them.

### v19–21 — The Whole Congregation Answers in Praise

The song returns briefly to the sea, where Pharaoh's horses, chariots, and horsemen entered and Israel passed through on dry land.

Then **Miriam the prophetess** takes a timbrel, and the women follow her with timbrels and dances.

She answers:

> “Sing ye to the LORD, for he hath triumphed gloriously; the horse and his rider hath he thrown into the sea.”

The victory is received through the whole congregation.

The people who had been organized by forced labor in Egypt are now gathered around the praise of the LORD who delivered them.

### v22–24 — Three Days Later, the Water Is Bitter

Moses brings Israel from the Red Sea into the wilderness of Shur.

> “And they went three days in the wilderness, and found no water.”

Then they come to **Marah**:

> “they could not drink of the waters of Marah, for they were bitter.”

The text itself explains the name through the bitterness of the water.

Only three days after the song of victory, the people murmur:

> **“What shall we drink?”**

The wilderness has exposed the next stage of their formation.

Israel knows that the LORD can defeat Pharaoh. Now the faculties must learn that **the same LORD can be trusted when the immediate need is water**.

The question has moved from deliverance to daily dependence.

### v25–26 — The LORD Heals the Bitter Water

The people murmur against Moses.

Moses:

> **“cried unto the LORD.”**

And:

> “the LORD shewed him a tree, which when he had cast into the waters, the waters were made sweet.”

The physical action carries the movement of the passage.

**The water is bitter.  
The LORD shows the remedy.  
The tree enters the water.  
The water becomes sweet.**

The condition Israel cannot use is healed by what God provides.

And there:

> “he made for them a statute and an ordinance, and there he proved them.”

Marah becomes a place of testing. The LORD calls Israel to hearken diligently to His voice, do what is right in His sight, and keep His commandments.

Then He reveals another dimension of His name and character:

> **“I am the LORD that healeth thee.”**

The God who saved Israel at the sea now teaches Israel to know Him as **the God who heals**.

The wilderness will require the people to carry their confidence in the LORD from one circumstance into the next.

### v27 — Elim

Then Israel comes to **Elim**:

> “where were twelve wells of water, and threescore and ten palm trees.”

And:

> “they encamped there by the waters.”

Marah is followed by Elim.

The people who asked, **“What shall we drink?”** now camp beside abundant water.

The twelve wells fit naturally within Israel's twelve-tribe structure, while the seventy palms recall the seventy descendants of Jacob who came into Egypt in Exodus 1:5. The text does not explicitly identify those correspondences as symbols, so they should be held as structural echoes rather than fixed equations.

The larger movement is clear in the narrative itself:

**the LORD delivers Israel, leads Israel into testing, heals what is bitter, and brings the people onward to provision.**

The wilderness is becoming the place where the delivered faculties learn what it means to trust their Deliverer.

## Fractal Notes & References

Exodus 15 establishes a pattern that will repeat throughout the wilderness: **God delivers, the people encounter need, the need exposes their present condition, they turn toward or murmur against the LORD, and God uses the circumstance to teach them His character and His order.** Exodus 16 will apply the same formation to food, and Exodus 17 to water and conflict. The wilderness progressively teaches Israel how to live under the God whose power they have already seen.

The song also reaches forward to the central purpose of Exodus. Israel sings of God's **“holy habitation”** and the **“Sanctuary”** before the Tabernacle has been built. Later the LORD will state the purpose plainly: **“I will dwell among the children of Israel, and will be their God”** (Exodus 29:45). The God who brings Israel through the sea intends to establish His Presence in the midst of the people.

The tree cast into the bitter waters also carries a natural Christian resonance. Scripture later places salvation through Jesus Christ upon the tree of the Cross, where death becomes the means through which life and healing are given. The immediate meaning in Exodus remains concrete: **God shows Moses what to put into the bitter water, and the water is healed.** Within the larger biblical pattern, the image accords with Christ entering the bitterness and death of the human condition and bringing life through His saving work.

| **Moses — Mosheh** | Exodus 2:10 connects the name with *mashah*, “to draw out” | **The Drawn-Out One**, now leading the delivered faculties through their first wilderness formation |
| **Israel — Yisrael** | Genesis 32:28 associates the name with striving with God and prevailing | **God-Prevails**; the faculties that have passed beyond Pharaoh's rule and are learning trust in the LORD |
| **Miriam — Miryam** | Exact etymology is uncertain; Scripture does not self-define the name | Functionally, **the prophetic voice of praise**, answering the LORD's deliverance with song |
| **Shur — Shur** | Commonly associated with “wall”; the exact origin of the place-name is uncertain | The wilderness into which the delivered people enter for their first sustained testing |
| **Marah — Marah** | Exodus 15:23 explicitly connects the name with *mar*, “bitter” | **Bitterness encountered on the journey**, where the faculties must learn to turn toward the LORD for healing |
| **Elim — Elim** | Often associated with large or strong trees; exact derivation is uncertain | **A station of water, shade, and provision** following the test at Marah |
| **Tree — Etz** | Ordinary Hebrew word for tree or wood | The physical means shown by God through which the bitter water is healed; within the wider biblical pattern, it carries a natural resonance with the Cross |
| **Healing — Rapha** | To heal, restore, or make whole | God's own declared action at Marah: **“I am the LORD that healeth thee”** |
| **Twelve Wells** | Twelve sources of water at Elim | A strong structural echo of **Israel's twelve-tribe organization**, with provision sufficient for the whole body |
