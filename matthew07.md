# MATTHEW 7

## 07 — THE HOUSE THAT WOULD NOT FALL

Jesus begins with a warning: "Judge not, that ye be not judged." He tells the disciples that the measure they use against others will be measured back to them. Then he gives them a picture of a man trying to remove a tiny mote from his brother's eye while a great beam remains in his own. The man is so occupied with correcting someone else that he has failed to see what is obstructing his own vision.

The disciple must begin with himself. It is easy to recognize pride, selfishness, dishonesty, or unbelief in another person while overlooking the same things in his own heart. Jesus tells him to remove the beam from his own eye first. Only then will he see clearly enough to help his brother. Notice that Jesus does not tell him to leave the mote forever. He is teaching him how to become capable of helping another person without blindness or hypocrisy.

Immediately afterward, Jesus tells the disciples not to give what is holy to dogs or cast their pearls before swine. This might seem surprising after his warning against judgment, but the two teachings belong together. Jesus is asking for discernment without hypocrisy. The disciple must learn to recognize what is precious and where it can be received. He should not treat the things of God carelessly, nor should he suppose that every person is ready to receive everything he has discovered. First he must see clearly, and then he must act with wisdom.

Jesus then turns to prayer. "Ask, and it shall be given you; seek, and ye shall find; knock, and it shall be opened unto you." He describes a father whose son asks for bread. Would he give him a stone? If the child asks for a fish, would he give him a serpent? Even an earthly father knows how to give good gifts to his children. How much more, Jesus asks, will the Father in heaven give good things to those who ask him?

The disciple should recognize the confidence Jesus is inviting him to have. He is not approaching an unwilling God who must be persuaded to care for him. He is coming to his Father. Jesus encourages him to ask, to seek, and to continue knocking. There is a relationship here, and the disciple must learn to trust the goodness of the One to whom he prays.

Then Jesus gives the rule that gathers up the way a man should treat others: "Therefore all things whatsoever ye would that men should do to you, do ye even so to them." The disciple is to begin acting toward others with the same consideration, mercy, and fairness that he desires for himself. The Word he has received must become visible in his relationships. Jesus says that this is the law and the prophets.

Now the teaching becomes more demanding. Jesus tells the disciples to enter through the strait gate, because the broad way leads to destruction, while the narrow way leads to life. Many travel the broad way, but few find the narrow one.

The disciple should understand that Jesus is describing a choice he must actually make. The broad way allows a man to continue with the habits, desires, and assumptions he has always followed. The narrow way requires him to hear what Jesus is saying and surrender whatever cannot pass through that gate. It may be easier to follow the crowd, to defend an old resentment, or to keep pursuing something he knows is wrong. But Jesus is calling him into a different way of living, and he must be willing to walk it even when others do not.

Jesus then warns against false prophets who come in sheep's clothing but are inwardly ravening wolves. He tells the disciples that they will know them by their fruits. A good tree brings forth good fruit, and a corrupt tree brings forth evil fruit. Grapes do not grow from thorns, nor figs from thistles.

Here Jesus gives the disciple a way to test what he hears. A person may speak beautiful words, claim spiritual authority, or appear outwardly righteous, but the fruit reveals what is actually growing within. The disciple must learn to look beyond appearances. Does the teaching produce the love, mercy, truthfulness, and obedience that Jesus himself has been teaching? Does the life of the teacher agree with his words? And perhaps most importantly, what kind of fruit is growing in the disciple's own life?

Jesus takes the warning further. Not everyone who calls him "Lord, Lord" will enter the kingdom of heaven, but the one who does the will of his Father. Some will point to prophecies, the casting out of devils, and wonderful works performed in his name. Yet Jesus says that he will declare to some of them, "I never knew you: depart from me, ye that work iniquity."

This is a searching warning for the serious disciple. A man may become interested in spiritual power, acquire great knowledge, or become known for remarkable works, and still neglect the very things Jesus has commanded him to do. Jesus is directing his attention beyond the appearance of spiritual accomplishment to the actual condition of his life. What matters is that he belongs to Christ and does the will of the Father. The disciple must be careful not to mistake religious activity for obedience.

Finally, Jesus gives them the image that brings the whole sermon to its conclusion.

A wise man builds his house upon a rock. The rain descends, the floods come, and the winds beat upon the house, but it does not fall because it is founded upon the rock. A foolish man builds his house upon the sand. The same rain, floods, and winds strike that house, and it falls with a great fall.

The two men hear the same words. Both build houses, and both encounter storms. Jesus tells us exactly what makes them different. The wise man hears his sayings and does them, while the foolish man hears them and does not do them.

This is where the disciple must examine his own foundation. He may have listened to the teachings of Jesus, understood their meaning, and even spoken about them to others. But has he begun to live according to them? Has he forgiven those who offended him? Has he learned to approach his Father in trust? Has he removed the beam from his own eye? Has he chosen the narrow way when the broad way seemed easier?

Jesus has spent three chapters teaching the disciple how to live in the kingdom of heaven. Now he tells him what will happen when that teaching is tested. The storm exposes what the house has been built upon. The disciple who has put the words of Jesus into practice discovers that his foundation is stronger than the circumstances beating against him.

The chapter ends with the multitude astonished at his doctrine, because he teaches as one having authority, and not as the scribes. Jesus has not merely offered another interpretation of the law. He has spoken with the authority of the one who knows the Father and calls men to build their lives upon his words.

## The Savior Test

Jesus gives us the test in his own words:

> "Therefore whosoever heareth these sayings of mine, and doeth them, I will liken him unto a wise man, which built his house upon a rock." — Matthew 7:24

This is the key to the entire chapter, and to the Sermon on the Mount. Jesus has been teaching the disciple about judgment, forgiveness, prayer, trust, desire, and righteousness. Now he tells him what to do with everything he has heard. He must put it into practice.

Jesus does not leave us to guess what he means by the rock. He identifies the wise builder as the man who hears his sayings and does them. The foundation is established as the disciple receives the words of Christ and begins to live by them.

Jesus also says:

> "Ye shall know them by their fruits." — Matthew 7:16

This confirms the test. The truth received within a man must eventually bear fruit in his life. A good tree produces good fruit because of the life working within it. The disciple should therefore examine what his beliefs are actually producing. He may call Jesus Lord, but is he following what Jesus has taught?

And Jesus gives the warning:

> "Not every one that saith unto me, Lord, Lord, shall enter into the kingdom of heaven; but he that doeth the will of my Father which is in heaven." — Matthew 7:21

There is an important distinction here. Jesus is not asking the disciple to earn the Father's love by accumulating religious achievements. He is warning him against claiming a relationship with Christ while refusing the very life Christ is teaching him to live. The disciple must become a doer of the Word.

The storm makes this truth unmistakable. Both houses are tested by the same forces. The difference becomes visible when the rain descends and the floods arise. The man who has built upon the rock stands, while the man who has built upon the sand discovers that his foundation cannot support him.

The disciple should ask himself a simple question: Am I merely admiring the words of Jesus, or am I building my life upon them?

The Sermon on the Mount ends with an invitation to begin building. Every act of forgiveness, every prayer offered in trust, every honest examination of the heart, and every choice to obey what Jesus has taught belongs to that work.

The storm will come. Jesus has already told the disciple how to build a house that will stand.
