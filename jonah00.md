# JONAH 00

## THE UNFINISHED PROPHET

*Call, Descent, Return, and the Deeper Work Still Remaining*

---

## BKV Insight

Jonah begins with a command:

> **“Arise, go to Nineveh, that great city.”**

The prophet rises.

Then he goes the other way.

That simple contradiction opens the entire book.

Jonah has genuinely heard the word of the LORD. His problem is not inability to recognize the call. He knows where he has been sent, and he deliberately moves away from it.

From that moment the story begins to descend.

Jonah goes **down to Joppa**.

He goes **down into the ship**.

He goes farther down into its sides and falls asleep.

He is cast into the sea.

In his prayer he remembers going down to **“the bottoms of the mountains.”**

The earth seems to close its bars around him.

Then the direction reverses:

> **“yet hast thou brought up my life from corruption, O LORD my God.”**

The fish returns Jonah to dry land.

And the word of the LORD comes again.

> **“Arise, go unto Nineveh.”**

This time Jonah arises and goes.

The first great movement of Jonah is therefore remarkably clear:

**CALL
→ CONTRARY MOVEMENT
→ DESCENT
→ CRISIS
→ DEATH-LIKE CONFINEMENT
→ PRAYER AND REMEMBRANCE
→ DELIVERANCE
→ SECOND CALL
→ OBEDIENCE**

If Jonah ended with Chapter 3, that would already make a complete story.

The prophet ran.

God recovered him.

The prophet obeyed.

Nineveh repented.

Judgment was withheld.

But Jonah has four chapters.

Chapter 4 reveals why.

Jonah's **direction** has changed.

His **inner government** has not yet fully changed with it.

He has walked to the city God chose. He has spoken the message God gave him. The people have responded. Yet when God shows mercy, Jonah becomes angry.

This gives the book a second movement.

Jonah first has to be brought back to the **commission**.

Then Jonah has to be confronted about the **heart from which he carries the commission**.

The second problem is deeper because Jonah can no longer solve it by changing his geographic direction. He is now outwardly where he was told to be.

The resistance has moved inside.

That is where Jonah becomes an especially powerful book for the disciple.

A person can hear God correctly.

A person can survive the consequences of going the wrong direction.

A person can pray from the depths.

A person can return.

A person can obey.

And after all of that, another layer of the old government can still remain.

Jonah is therefore a book of **restored obedience followed by interior exposure**.

The LORD rescues the prophet from the sea.

Then He begins exposing the prophet who came out of it.

## Canonical History & Context

Jonah is identified as:

> **“Jonah the son of Amittai.”**

The same prophet appears outside this book in **2 Kings 14:25**, where Jonah son of Amittai from **Gath-hepher** delivers a word concerning the restoration of Israel's border during the reign of **Jeroboam II**.

That places Jonah's known prophetic ministry in the world of the northern kingdom of Israel during the **eighth century BC**.

This matters.

Jonah is already a prophet of Israel before the LORD sends him to Nineveh. His mission to the foreign city does not begin with an inexperienced man trying to discover whether God has spoken. He is an established prophet receiving a difficult commission.

### Israel and Assyria

Nineveh was a major city of **Assyria**, the great Mesopotamian power that would increasingly dominate the region.

For an Israelite prophet, Nineveh was therefore no neutral destination.

Jonah is being sent beyond Israel toward a great foreign city whose wickedness has:

> **“come up before”**

the LORD.

The book never needs to turn Nineveh into an abstract symbol. Its historical function already carries enormous weight.

It is a foreign city.

Its wickedness has reached God.

Judgment is announced against it.

Yet its people prove capable of hearing the warning, humbling themselves, turning from violence, and receiving mercy.

### The Geography of the Flight

Jonah is commanded to go to **Nineveh**, inland in Assyria, far to Israel's east and northeast.

Instead he goes to **Joppa**, the Mediterranean port, finds a ship bound for **Tarshish**, pays the fare, and begins a westward sea journey.

The precise ancient location of Tarshish remains uncertain. Its narrative function is much clearer than its exact coordinates.

**Tarshish is where Jonah is going because Nineveh is where God told him to go.**

The geography therefore participates in the story.

Jonah does not merely delay the commission.

He chooses another direction.

And as he moves away from the call, the repeated language of the chapter carries him **down**.

## The Shape of Jonah

Jonah is only four chapters long, but it contains two closely related movements.

### Movement One — The Prophet's Direction Is Corrected

#### 1. The Word Comes

The book begins with divine initiative:

> **“Now the word of the LORD came unto Jonah…”**

Jonah does not invent his mission.

He receives it.

The command is:

**Arise
→ go
→ cry against Nineveh.**

The entire first half of the book develops from Jonah's response to those verbs.

### 2. Jonah Arises in the Wrong Direction

Jonah does technically arise.

But he rises:

> **“to flee unto Tarshish from the presence of the LORD.”**

This is important.

Activity alone does not equal obedience.

Jonah is moving decisively.

He knows what he wants.

He spends money.

He finds transportation.

He begins a journey.

His movement is organized and intentional.

It is also contrary to the word he has received.

### 3. The Descent Begins

The narrative then keeps carrying Jonah downward.

He goes down to Joppa.

He goes down into the ship.

He goes down into its interior.

He goes down into sleep.

Eventually he goes down into the sea.

In Chapter 2 he describes the descent reaching:

> **“the bottoms of the mountains.”**

The progression is too concentrated to ignore.

Jonah's flight is spatially represented as a descent.

### 4. The Nations Begin Responding Before Jonah Does

The sailors are foreigners.

At the beginning of the storm they cry every man unto his own god.

Yet as the crisis develops, something remarkable happens.

The pagan shipmaster has to wake the Hebrew prophet and tell him:

> **“Arise, call upon thy God.”**

The language of Jonah's commission is now echoing back to him through a foreign sailor.

By the end of Chapter 1, the sailors:

> **“feared the LORD exceedingly”**

and offered sacrifice and made vows.

Jonah has refused to carry the word of the LORD to Gentiles in Nineveh.

Yet Gentiles are already beginning to respond to the LORD around him.

That pattern will become even larger in Chapter 3.

### 5. Jonah Enters Death-Like Confinement

Jonah is cast into the sea.

The storm ceases.

Then the LORD prepares a great fish.

The fish preserves Jonah, but the experience is described in the language of death.

Jonah cries:

> **“out of the belly of hell.”**

Waters surround him.

The depth closes around him.

Weeds wrap around his head.

He descends beneath the mountains.

The earth closes its bars around him.

Jonah is alive, yet the narrative places him inside the architecture of burial.

That is why Jesus can later take Jonah's three days inside the creature of the deep and identify them with His own descent into the heart of the earth.

### 6. Jonah Remembers the LORD

Inside this confinement Jonah prays.

His prayer draws heavily upon the language and world of the Psalms: waters, depths, waves, distress, the holy temple, remembered prayer, deliverance, thanksgiving, vows.

At the center comes a turning of attention:

> **“When my soul fainted within me I remembered the LORD.”**

Then Jonah declares:

> **“Salvation is of the LORD.”**

The narrative does not give us a long confession in which Jonah analyzes all of his motives.

It gives us something more concrete.

The prophet at the bottom remembers God.

He prays.

He looks again toward the holy temple.

He acknowledges the LORD as the source of salvation.

And God brings him back.

### 7. The Prophet Returns to Dry Land

The LORD speaks to the fish.

Jonah is returned to dry land.

The sea that marked his descent no longer holds him.

The first major movement has reached its reversal:

**down
→ depth
→ confinement
→ prayer
→ brought up
→ dry land**

The prophet has passed through an experience resembling death and restoration.

Now comes the decisive test.

Will he answer the call differently when it comes again?

### 8. The Word Comes a Second Time

Chapter 3 begins almost as Chapter 1 began:

> **“And the word of the LORD came unto Jonah the second time.”**

Again:

> **“Arise, go unto Nineveh.”**

This repetition is one of the governing structures of the book.

The commission has survived Jonah's flight.

Jonah's failure did not destroy God's purpose.

The word returns.

And this time:

> **“Jonah arose, and went unto Nineveh, according to the word of the LORD.”**

The direction has been corrected.

### 9. Nineveh Turns

Jonah enters the city and announces coming overthrow.

The response is extraordinary.

The people believe God.

They fast.

They put on sackcloth.

The king comes down from his throne, removes his robe, covers himself with sackcloth, and sits in ashes.

A city facing judgment changes direction.

The king commands:

> **“let them turn every one from his evil way, and from the violence that is in their hands.”**

Then:

> **“God saw their works, that they turned from their evil way.”**

Nineveh's turning answers Jonah's earlier contrary movement in an unexpected way.

The prophet of Israel had received God's word and turned away from his assignment.

The Gentile city receives God's warning and turns away from its evil.

Jonah has finally gone in the right direction.

Now Nineveh does too.

And God spares the city.

That should be the happy ending.

Instead it opens the deepest conflict in the book.

---

### Movement Two — The Prophet's Inner Government Is Exposed

Chapter 4 begins:

> **“But it displeased Jonah exceedingly, and he was very angry.”**

Nineveh's repentance has succeeded.

Jonah's preaching has succeeded.

The announced destruction has been withheld.

And the prophet is furious.

Now Jonah finally tells us why he fled in Chapter 1.

He says he knew all along that the LORD was:

> **“a gracious God, and merciful, slow to anger, and of great kindness.”**

This reaches directly back into one of Scripture's central revelations of God's character.

When the LORD revealed Himself to Moses in Exodus 34, He proclaimed Himself merciful, gracious, longsuffering, and abundant in goodness and truth.

Jonah knows this revelation.

That is precisely his problem.

He suspected that if Nineveh turned, God might show Nineveh the same mercy Scripture says belongs to His character.

Jonah's problem therefore reaches deeper than fear.

He did not flee because he thought God might fail.

He fled at least partly because he feared God might act exactly like God.

Now the architecture of the whole book becomes visible.

Jonah needed his **feet** turned toward Nineveh.

He still needs his **judgment** brought into agreement with God.

### The Plant and the Prophet

Jonah leaves the city and sits on its east side.

He makes himself a booth and waits to see what will happen.

The LORD prepares a plant that rises over Jonah and gives shade.

Jonah is exceedingly glad for it.

Then God prepares a worm.

The plant withers.

God sends a vehement east wind.

The sun beats upon Jonah.

The prophet again asks to die.

The great prophet who survived the sea is now undone by the loss of a plant.

Yet the plant has revealed something the storm could not.

Jonah cares deeply about mercy when Jonah needs it.

He welcomed preservation in the fish.

He welcomed deliverance onto dry land.

He welcomes shade from the plant.

His difficulty appears when divine mercy extends toward Nineveh.

The plant therefore functions as a teaching instrument.

It gives Jonah a small experience of attachment, loss, pity, and exposure through which God can question the scale of Jonah's judgment.

### The Book Ends with a Question

God asks Jonah whether his anger is right.

Jonah insists that it is.

Then the LORD places the plant beside Nineveh.

Jonah has pity concerning a plant that appeared quickly and disappeared quickly.

God points toward the great city, filled with human beings and animals, and asks:

> **“And should not I spare Nineveh…?”**

The book ends there.

Jonah does not answer.

That absence is part of the architecture.

The final question has moved beyond Jonah.

It reaches the reader.

The first word of the book calls Jonah to move.

The final word asks Jonah how he sees.

The prophet's geographic journey is complete.

His interior journey is left open.

## Canonical Connections

### Exodus — The God Jonah Already Knows

One of Jonah's most important canonical connections appears in Chapter 4.

Jonah describes the LORD as:

**gracious
→ merciful
→ slow to anger
→ abundant in kindness
→ willing to withhold announced destruction**

This is the language of **Exodus 34**, where God reveals His name and character to Moses after Israel's great covenant failure.

That connection changes how Jonah's flight should be understood.

Jonah is not learning for the first time that God is merciful.

He already knows.

His struggle is whether he agrees with that mercy when it reaches a people he would rather see judged.

That makes Jonah a book about something deeper than theological knowledge.

A person can know the revelation correctly and still resist living under its government.

### Exodus — Through the Waters

Jonah also contains a smaller passage-through-the-waters structure.

The sea becomes the place of crisis.

Jonah enters it.

God preserves life through it.

Jonah emerges onto dry land.

The correspondence with Exodus should remain functional rather than forced. Jonah is not reenacting every feature of the Red Sea crossing.

The shared architecture is sufficient:

**threatening waters
→ divine intervention
→ passage through what should destroy
→ dry land
→ renewed movement under God's command**

The sailors also move from terror toward the fear of the LORD, sacrifice, and vows.

The sea has become a revelation.

### Genesis — Waters, Dry Land, and a New Beginning

Genesis repeatedly uses the appearance of dry land after overwhelming waters as a movement toward renewed life.

Creation itself distinguishes waters and dry land.

After the Flood, Noah eventually emerges into a world where the waters have receded.

Jonah's return to dry land belongs naturally within that larger biblical vocabulary.

Again, the events are different.

The function is related.

**Life comes through the waters and receives ground upon which a new movement can begin.**

Jonah receives that ground immediately before the word of the LORD comes again.

### Genesis 4 — Anger Under Divine Questioning

A more subtle correspondence appears near Jonah's ending.

God asks Cain:

> **“Why art thou wroth?”**

God asks Jonah:

> **“Doest thou well to be angry?”**

Both questions address anger before God.

Both force the person to examine an inward government rather than merely an outward circumstance.

Cain later dwells east of Eden.

Jonah sits east of Nineveh.

The eastern geography makes the correspondence interesting, though it should remain secondary.

The stronger link is functional:

**anger has taken the governing position, and God questions the one who believes the anger is justified.**

### Elijah — The Prophet Who Wants to Die

Jonah 4 strongly recalls Elijah in 1 Kings 19.

Elijah journeys into the wilderness, sits beneath a tree, and asks God to take his life.

Jonah sits outside Nineveh beneath temporary shade and repeatedly says that death would be better than life.

In each story the prophet has already participated in a major divine work.

The crisis comes afterward.

That is important.

Great spiritual events do not automatically settle every interior conflict in the person who carries them.

The prophet himself can still require God's ministry, questioning, correction, and provision.

### Jeremiah — The Word That Must Be Spoken

The calling pattern of Jeremiah gives another useful comparison.

God's prophet is not free to replace the divine message with a more comfortable one.

The prophetic office receives a word and carries it where God sends it.

Jonah dramatizes what happens when the messenger attempts to separate himself from that function.

The second commission brings the role back into simple form:

> **“preach unto it the preaching that I bid thee.”**

Jonah's authority lies in carrying the word he is given.

### Jeremiah 18 — A Nation Can Turn

Jeremiah later gives explicit prophetic form to what Jonah narrates.

If God announces judgment concerning a nation and that nation turns from its evil, God may withhold the announced evil.

That is almost exactly the functional structure of Jonah 3.

Nineveh hears.

Nineveh turns.

God sees the turning.

The announced overthrow is withheld.

Jonah therefore shows in narrative form a principle that Jeremiah states directly.

### The Psalms — Prayer from the Depths

Jonah's prayer sounds at home inside the Psalter.

The waters come around his soul.

The deep surrounds him.

Waves and billows pass over him.

He cries from distress.

His prayer reaches God's holy place.

He remembers the LORD.

He gives thanks for deliverance.

Psalm 42 says:

> **“all thy waves and thy billows are gone over me.”**

Jonah uses the same world of prayer.

This is significant for the interior reading because Jonah does not escape the deep by pretending he is somewhere else.

He prays from within it.

The depth becomes a place from which relationship with God is recovered.

### Joel — Turn Because God Is Merciful

Joel calls the people to turn to the LORD because He is:

**gracious
merciful
slow to anger
of great kindness**

Jonah uses the same divine character almost as a complaint.

That contrast is remarkable.

Joel hears God's mercy and says:

**therefore return.**

Jonah knows God's mercy and says, in effect:

**that is why I ran.**

The same revelation can therefore produce very different responses depending upon what governs the hearer.

### Jesus — The Sign of Jonah

Jesus gives Jonah an explicit place in the Gospel architecture.

In Matthew 12 He connects Jonah's:

> **“three days and three nights”**

inside the great fish with the Son of Man being:

> **“three days and three nights in the heart of the earth.”**

This establishes the descent-and-return pattern beyond reasonable doubt.

Jonah passes through a death-shaped confinement and emerges alive.

Jesus identifies that form with His own death and resurrection.

But Jesus also emphasizes another part of Jonah.

The people of Nineveh:

> **“repented at the preaching of Jonas.”**

The sign of Jonah therefore includes more than the fish.

It includes proclamation reaching a people capable of responding.

The one delivered from death carries a word that confronts others with the possibility of turning.

### Acts — The Boundary Opens Toward the Nations

Jonah and Acts are not the same story, yet an important canonical movement appears in both.

In Jonah, Gentiles repeatedly respond to the God of Israel.

The sailors move toward the fear of the LORD.

Nineveh believes God.

In Acts, the movement toward the nations expands dramatically, and Peter himself must learn that God's action is already reaching people beyond the boundaries he had expected.

The correspondence is especially useful at the functional level:

**God's mercy reaches farther than the messenger's inherited categories.**

Sometimes the messenger must be transformed by the very mission he was sent to carry.

Jonah shows how difficult that transformation can be.

## Interior Architecture

Jonah gives the disciple one of Scripture's clearest pictures of a call that must penetrate more than one layer of the person.

The governing question is:

**What happens when a person receives a genuine divine call, moves in the opposite direction, reaches the limit of that movement, is restored to the call, obeys outwardly, and then discovers that the deeper resistance still remains?**

### 1. The Call Is Real

The journey begins when something is actually known.

The word comes.

There is a direction.

There is something to do.

There are moments in the life of the disciple when the real difficulty is not uncertainty.

We know.

Jonah knows.

### 2. The Contrary Direction Can Be Highly Organized

Jonah's resistance does not look passive.

He gets up.

He travels.

He finds the ship.

He pays.

He leaves.

That is an important interior observation.

A person can expend enormous energy moving away from something already known to be right.

The wrong direction may be efficient.

It may have transportation.

It may have a schedule.

It may even feel like decisive action.

The governing question remains:

**In what direction is the movement carrying the life?**

### 3. Descent Eventually Reveals the Limit of Self-Direction

Jonah keeps going down until there is nowhere left to navigate.

At first he chooses the road.

Then he chooses the ship.

Eventually the storm takes over.

Then the sailors lift him.

Then the sea receives him.

Then the fish receives him.

Jonah gradually loses control over the movement he began.

This gives the descent its interior force.

A contrary direction can begin as personal choice and end in a place where the person no longer possesses the power to restore himself.

That is where Jonah says:

> **“Salvation is of the LORD.”**

### 4. The Depth Can Become the Place of Remembering

The fish is terrifying.

It is also preservation.

The same event can carry both functions.

Jonah has reached a death-like place, yet he is still alive enough to pray.

When his soul faints:

> **“I remembered the LORD.”**

The deepest point of Jonah's descent therefore becomes the point at which his attention turns again toward God.

For the disciple, this is one of the book's strongest patterns.

The depth does not have to become the final identity.

It can become the place from which God is remembered.

### 5. Deliverance Returns the Disciple to the Call

Jonah does not emerge from the fish into retirement.

The word returns.

The same commission is waiting.

This is a powerful picture of restoration.

Grace does more than remove Jonah from distress.

Grace restores Jonah to purpose.

The second word of the LORD proves that failure has not been given final authority over the calling.

### 6. Obedience Is Real Progress

Jonah goes to Nineveh.

That matters.

The BKV reading should not minimize this because Chapter 4 later exposes him.

A man who previously fled now obeys.

The direction has changed.

The message is delivered.

A city responds.

This is genuine movement.

Yet genuine movement can reveal the next thing that must be transformed.

### 7. Success Exposes What Failure Hid

While Jonah is fleeing, the obvious problem is disobedience.

Once Jonah obeys, another problem becomes visible.

He does not want the mission to produce the result God's mercy permits.

This is a much subtler form of resistance.

The disciple may eventually discover:

**I obeyed God, but I still wanted God to produce my preferred outcome.**

Jonah's anger exposes that distinction.

### 8. The Old Government Can Survive Inside New Behavior

Jonah now does the correct thing outwardly.

But his measure of judgment remains active within him.

He is willing to receive mercy when he is drowning.

He struggles to rejoice when mercy reaches Nineveh.

That is why Chapter 4 belongs to the book.

The first transformation changes Jonah's direction.

The second transformation concerns what rules Jonah after he has arrived.

This pattern appears throughout Scripture.

Israel can leave Egypt while Egypt's habits remain active in the wilderness.

A person can cross a boundary before the inner kingdom has learned how to live on the other side.

Deliverance is real.

Further transformation is also real.

### 9. The Plant Reveals Attachment

God gives Jonah shade.

Jonah rejoices.

God removes the shade.

Jonah collapses into anger and death-wish.

The plant has become a remarkably precise diagnostic.

Jonah's reaction reveals the scale of his attachments.

He feels intensely for the loss of something that served him for a single day.

God then directs his attention toward an entire city.

The issue is no longer whether Jonah knows the law.

It is whether his sense of value is being brought into alignment with God's.

### 10. Divine Questioning Opens the Final Door

God could end Jonah by issuing another command.

Instead He asks a question.

That matters.

The question brings Jonah into a place where obedience alone cannot finish the work.

He must see.

He must recognize.

He must decide whether his own judgment or God's mercy will define reality.

Scripture never records his answer.

Perhaps the disciple is meant to supply it.

One of Jonah's most revealing self-descriptions occurs aboard the ship:

> **“I fear the LORD, the God of heaven, which hath made the sea and the dry land.”**

Jonah's theology is again correct.

He serves the God who made **the sea and the dry land** while attempting to cross the sea to escape His commission.

The book repeatedly closes the distance between what Jonah knows and what Jonah is willing to live.

That may be the deepest significance of Jonah son of Amittai.

The prophet knows truth.

The question is whether truth has reached the government of the whole man.

## The Book in One Movement

Jonah begins when the word of the LORD enters a man's life.

The man hears it clearly.

Then he moves against it.

His contrary movement becomes a descent.

He goes down to the port, down into the ship, down into sleep, down into the sea, and finally down beneath the mountains until the earth seems to close over him.

There, at the limit of his own direction, Jonah remembers the LORD.

He prays.

God brings his life up.

The prophet returns to dry land.

Then the word comes again.

This time Jonah rises and goes where he was sent.

A foreign city hears.

The king leaves his throne.

Violence is abandoned.

The threatened judgment is withheld.

And suddenly the deepest resistance in Jonah finally becomes visible.

The prophet who could survive the sea cannot bear mercy shown to Nineveh.

So God gives him a plant.

Then a worm.

Then an east wind.

Then a question.

That is the full movement of Jonah:

**THE WORD
→ THE CONTRARY WAY
→ THE DESCENT
→ THE DEPTH
→ THE CRY
→ THE RETURN
→ THE SECOND WORD
→ THE RIGHT DIRECTION
→ THE TURNING OF NINEVEH
→ THE MERCY OF GOD
→ THE ANGER OF THE PROPHET
→ THE EXPOSURE
→ THE QUESTION**

The fish is therefore not the end of Jonah's transformation.

It gets Jonah back onto dry land.

Nineveh gets Jonah back into his commission.

Chapter 4 goes after something deeper.

The LORD is bringing the prophet toward agreement with the heart of the One whose word he carries.

And Scripture closes the book before telling us whether Jonah gets there.

The final step is left open.

The word has reached the prophet.

The prophet has reached Nineveh.

Now the mercy of God must reach the prophet.

**That is where the Book of Jonah leaves us.**
