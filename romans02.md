# ROMANS 2

## 02 — THE JUDGE IS JUDGED

Paul has just described what happens when men turn away from the truth of God and allow their desires to govern them. His account is severe, and anyone reading it might be tempted to agree with every word while thinking about the faults of other people. But now Paul turns directly toward that reader. "Therefore thou art inexcusable, O man, whosoever thou art that judgest."

This is an unexpected turn. The man who was ready to condemn the sins of others discovers that Paul is speaking to him. He may recognize unrighteousness when he sees it, but he has not necessarily dealt with the same unrighteousness in his own heart. Paul tells him that when he judges another while doing the same things, he condemns himself.

The disciple should pay attention to this. There is a difference between recognizing what is wrong and imagining that he is righteous because he can recognize it. A man may speak strongly against dishonesty while making excuses for his own. He may condemn pride in another person while being deeply offended whenever his own importance is questioned. He may even become so occupied with correcting the world that he neglects the very work God is calling him to do within himself.

Paul then speaks of the goodness, forbearance, and longsuffering of God. He asks whether the man despises these riches, not knowing that the goodness of God leads him to repentance.

This is one of the most important statements in the chapter. God has shown patience toward the man, but the man has misunderstood that patience. Instead of allowing God's goodness to bring him to repentance, he has used the time to continue defending himself.

The disciple should recognize that repentance is more than admitting that he has made a mistake. It begins when he becomes willing to turn from the thinking and conduct that have separated him from the truth. The goodness of God gives him the opportunity to do this. He need not spend his life defending what he has done or proving himself better than other men. He can acknowledge what is wrong and begin to walk differently.

Paul warns that a hard and unrepentant heart is storing up wrath against the day of judgment. God will render to every man according to his deeds, and there is no respect of persons with Him.

The disciple must understand what this means for the confidence he has placed in outward things. He cannot rely upon his reputation, his religious history, or his ability to explain the Scriptures while refusing to live according to the truth he knows. God sees the man himself. The judgment Paul describes reaches the actual life, not merely the account a man gives of that life.

Then Paul makes a distinction between hearing the law and doing it. "For not the hearers of the law are just before God, but the doers of the law shall be justified."

A person may have heard the commandments from childhood, know their meaning, and even teach them accurately. Yet none of that guarantees that he has allowed them to govern his life. The disciple should recognize the danger of becoming familiar with the Word without being changed by it. He may know what forgiveness means and still refuse to forgive. He may understand the command to love his neighbour and still treat people with contempt.

Paul then speaks of the Gentiles, who do not possess the law in the same way as the Jews, yet sometimes do by nature the things contained in it. He says that they show the work of the law written in their hearts, their conscience bearing witness, and their thoughts accusing or excusing one another.

This reveals something important about the inner life. God has not left man without a witness. Even where the written law has not been received, conscience bears testimony concerning right and wrong. A man may try to silence that witness, explain it away, or become accustomed to ignoring it, but Paul points to something deeper than outward religious instruction.

The disciple should examine how he responds when his conscience bears witness against something he has done. Does he listen and allow himself to be corrected? Or does he immediately begin forming arguments to justify himself? The truth may be uncomfortable, but the willingness to receive correction is part of the life God is developing within him.

Paul says that God will judge the secrets of men by Jesus Christ. The judgment reaches the things that other people cannot see. The hidden motives, private decisions, and thoughts a man has carefully concealed are not hidden from God.

Then Paul turns specifically to the Jew who rests in the law, boasts of knowing God's will, and considers himself a guide to the blind and a teacher of the foolish. These are great privileges and responsibilities. But Paul asks a devastating question: "Thou therefore which teachest another, teachest thou not thyself?"

Here the disciple encounters the central danger of religious knowledge. The man has become so familiar with teaching the law that he assumes he is already living according to it. He can instruct others while remaining unwilling to receive instruction himself.

Paul asks whether the one who preaches against stealing steals, whether the one who forbids adultery commits adultery, and whether the one who boasts of the law dishonours God by breaking it.

These questions are meant to reach the conscience. The disciple must be willing to apply to himself the truth he has been so ready to apply to others. The Word is not merely something he possesses and explains. It is something he must receive and obey.

Paul then turns to circumcision, the covenant sign given to Abraham and his descendants. He explains that circumcision is profitable if a man keeps the law, but if he breaks the law, his circumcision becomes uncircumcision. Conversely, the man who lacks the outward sign but fulfills the righteousness of the law exposes the inconsistency of the man who possesses the sign and disobeys.

The outward mark was real, and the covenant it signified was important. But Paul is showing that the sign cannot substitute for the inward life it was meant to express.

The disciple should understand the deeper meaning of this. A man may possess all the recognizable marks of religious belonging and still be governed by pride, envy, resentment, or selfish ambition. He may identify himself with the people of God while resisting the very change God desires to accomplish within him.

Circumcision represents a cutting away. Applied to the heart, it points toward the removal of what has been allowed to come between the man and God. The old attachments, selfish desires, and stubborn resistance must be dealt with inwardly. The disciple cannot accomplish this by altering his outward appearance or acquiring a religious identity. Paul directs him toward the work of the Spirit.

The chapter reaches its conclusion with an extraordinary declaration:

"But he is a Jew, which is one inwardly; and circumcision is that of the heart, in the spirit, and not in the letter; whose praise is not of men, but of God."

Paul has brought the disciple all the way from judging the outward sins of others to examining the hidden condition of his own heart.

The man who began the chapter confident that he could identify unrighteousness must now ask whether he is willing to be made righteous himself. The religious teacher must become a disciple. The man who possesses the outward sign must receive the inward work. And the man who has been seeking the approval of others must learn to seek the praise that comes from God.

## The Savior Test

Jesus gives us the first test when he speaks about judging others:

> "For with what judgment ye judge, ye shall be judged: and with what measure ye mete, it shall be measured to you again." — Matthew 7:2

This confirms the warning with which Paul opens Romans 2. The man who judges his brother must first examine himself. Jesus gives the picture of a man trying to remove a mote from another person's eye while a beam remains in his own. He tells him to remove the beam first, so that he may see clearly enough to help his brother.

Jesus is not forbidding the disciple to recognize evil. He is teaching him to begin with his own heart. The man who is unwilling to be corrected cannot safely make himself the judge of everyone else.

Paul then speaks of those who possess the law and teach it to others without obeying it themselves. Jesus confirms the same danger:

> "For they say, and do not." — Matthew 23:3

Jesus is speaking about the scribes and Pharisees who teach the law while failing to live according to their own instruction. The disciple must recognize that knowledge of Scripture carries a responsibility to put that knowledge into practice. He cannot become content with knowing what God requires while continually excusing himself from doing it.

Then Jesus gives a particularly direct confirmation of Paul's teaching about circumcision of the heart:

> "Thou blind Pharisee, cleanse first that which is within the cup and platter, that the outside of them may be clean also." — Matthew 23:26

Paul says that true circumcision is inward, in the heart and in the Spirit. Jesus makes the same distinction between outward religious appearance and inward cleansing. A man may appear righteous to others while carrying corruption within. Jesus directs him to begin where the trouble actually lies.

This gives us the key to the whole chapter. The disciple must allow the Word of God to reach the things he has concealed beneath his outward life. He must be willing to examine the motives behind his actions, the pride behind his judgments, and the desires he has continued to defend.

Jesus also tells us how to recognize the life of a man:

> "Wherefore by their fruits ye shall know them." — Matthew 7:20

The fruit reveals what is growing within. The disciple should examine what his knowledge of God is producing. Has it made him more merciful, more truthful, more willing to forgive, and more obedient to the Father? Or has it become a means of placing himself above others?

Finally, Jesus explains where the true praise must come from:

> "How can ye believe, which receive honour one of another, and seek not the honour that cometh from God only?" — John 5:44

This confirms Paul's closing words about the man whose praise is not of men, but of God.

The disciple may seek to be recognized as knowledgeable, faithful, or spiritually mature. But Jesus calls him away from the desire to be admired and toward the actual life of faith. He must become willing to be corrected, cleansed, and changed where only God can see.

Romans 2 asks the disciple to turn the light upon himself. He has seen the corruption described in Romans 1, and now he must be willing to recognize whatever remains of it within his own heart.

The Savior Test is simple. Before correcting another man, examine yourself. Before teaching the Word, receive it. Before trusting an outward sign of righteousness, allow Christ to cleanse what is within.

The judge must first become the disciple.
