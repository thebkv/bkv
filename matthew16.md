# MATTHEW 16

## 16 — THE ROCK AND THE KEYS

The Pharisees and Sadducees come to Jesus asking for a sign from heaven. They have already witnessed his works, yet they still demand another demonstration before they will believe him. Jesus tells them that they can read the appearance of the sky and predict the weather, but they cannot discern the signs of the times. They have become skilled at interpreting what they see in the natural world while remaining blind to the presence of God standing before them.

Jesus refuses their demand and tells them that no sign will be given except the sign of the prophet Jonah. The disciple should recognize the warning. It is possible to keep asking God for further proof while refusing to receive what he has already revealed. Jesus has been healing the sick, feeding the multitudes, and demonstrating the authority of the kingdom. Yet the religious leaders continue to look elsewhere for their answer. The sign of Jonah points toward the death and resurrection of Jesus, when the truth they have refused to recognize will be revealed in a way they never expected.

When the disciples cross the sea, they discover that they have forgotten to bring bread. Jesus warns them to beware of the leaven of the Pharisees and Sadducees, but they assume he is speaking about their lack of food. He reminds them of the feeding of the five thousand and the four thousand. They have seen him provide bread in abundance, yet their thoughts immediately return to their outward circumstances.

Then they understand that he is warning them about the doctrine of the Pharisees and Sadducees.

This is an important distinction for the disciple. The bread that nourishes the body is not the same as the teaching that enters the mind. A small amount of leaven spreads through the whole lump of dough. In the same way, a false assumption about God can spread through a man's thinking until it influences everything he believes. The Pharisees and Sadducees represent different religious approaches, yet both can become obstacles when human teaching takes the place of what God is revealing. Jesus is teaching the disciple to examine what he receives and what it is producing within him.

Then Jesus brings the disciples into the region of Caesarea Philippi and asks a question that changes the course of the chapter: "Whom do men say that I the Son of man am?"

The disciples repeat what others have been saying. Some believe he is John the Baptist, others Elijah, Jeremiah, or one of the prophets. There are many opinions about Jesus, and some are deeply respectful, but none reaches the truth of who he is.

Jesus turns the question directly upon them: "But whom say ye that I am?"

Peter answers, "Thou art the Christ, the Son of the living God."

Jesus immediately tells Peter that flesh and blood has not revealed this to him, but the Father in heaven. Peter has received something beyond the opinions of other men. He has recognized the Christ because the Father has made him known.

This is the great turning point of the chapter. The disciple may spend years hearing what other people believe about Jesus, learning their explanations, and comparing their conclusions. But Jesus brings him to a question that must be answered within his own heart. Who is Jesus to him? Peter's confession is more than a correct religious statement. Jesus identifies it as a revelation from the Father.

Then comes the promise of the rock and the keys.

Jesus tells Peter that upon this rock he will build his church, and the gates of hell shall not prevail against it. He promises him the keys of the kingdom of heaven and speaks of binding and loosing.

The disciple should pay close attention to the connection between Peter's confession and the promise that follows. Peter has recognized Jesus as the Christ, the Son of the living God. Jesus speaks of an assembly that will be built upon a foundation strong enough to withstand the powers of death. The rock has been understood in several closely related ways, including Peter himself, his confession, and Christ whom he confesses. But the disciple should not miss what the conversation reveals: the Father has made Christ known, and Jesus now speaks of building something that the gates of hell cannot overcome.

The keys also deserve attention. They speak of authority within the kingdom, not merely possession of religious information. Jesus gives Peter a responsibility connected with what is bound and loosed. The disciple should understand that knowing Christ brings him into a life of obedience and responsibility. What the Father reveals must begin to govern how he lives.

But immediately after this extraordinary revelation, the chapter takes a startling turn.

Jesus begins telling his disciples that he must go to Jerusalem, suffer many things, be killed, and rise again the third day. Peter takes him aside and begins to rebuke him, insisting that such things must never happen.

Only moments earlier, Peter had recognized the Christ through revelation from the Father. Now he is trying to prevent that same Christ from fulfilling the work he has come to accomplish.

Jesus turns and says, "Get thee behind me, Satan: thou art an offence unto me: for thou savourest not the things that be of God, but those that be of men."

This is one of the most searching moments in the Gospel. Peter loves Jesus and does not want him to suffer. His intention may seem affectionate and protective, but his thinking has become an obstacle to the divine purpose. He wants the glory of Christ without the suffering that Jesus says must come first.

The disciple must learn that a genuine revelation does not mean that every thought he subsequently has comes from God. Peter has received something true from the Father, yet he can still misunderstand how that truth must unfold. He must continue to listen to Jesus, especially when the way Jesus describes is different from the way he would have chosen.

Then Jesus gives the teaching that brings the entire chapter home to every disciple:

"If any man will come after me, let him deny himself, and take up his cross, and follow me."

The disciple has just watched Peter attempt to direct Jesus away from the cross. Now Jesus explains that the disciple himself must take up his own cross to follow him. He cannot simply recognize Christ and then expect Christ to conform to all his natural desires. There will be things he must surrender because they stand in the way of the life Jesus is leading him into.

Jesus goes further. Whoever tries to save his life will lose it, but whoever loses his life for Jesus' sake will find it. Then he asks what a man would profit if he gained the whole world but lost his own soul.

This is the same lesson Peter has just encountered. A man may want to preserve everything familiar, comfortable, and safe, but Jesus is calling him into a life that cannot be received by clinging to the old one. The disciple must be willing to follow Christ beyond the limits of his former understanding.

The chapter ends with Jesus speaking of the Son of man coming in the glory of his Father and rewarding every man according to his works. He also tells them that some standing there will not taste death until they see the Son of man coming in his kingdom. Matthew immediately follows this promise with the Transfiguration in chapter 17, where Peter, James, and John see Jesus revealed in glory.

The disciple should see the movement of the whole chapter. Peter first recognizes the Christ, then discovers that his natural thinking still resists the way of Christ. Jesus gives him the promise of the rock and the keys, but he also teaches him the necessity of the cross.

To know who Jesus is marks the beginning of a great discovery. To follow him requires the disciple to let that revelation change the way he thinks and lives.

## The Savior Test

Jesus gives us the first key when he responds to Peter's confession:

> "Blessed art thou, Simon Barjona: for flesh and blood hath not revealed it unto thee, but my Father which is in heaven." — Matthew 16:17

The disciple must recognize that Peter has received a revelation from the Father. He has moved beyond repeating what other men say about Jesus and has come to know him as the Christ, the Son of the living God.

Jesus then declares:

> "And I say also unto thee, That thou art Peter, and upon this rock I will build my church; and the gates of hell shall not prevail against it." — Matthew 16:18

The promise is extraordinary. Jesus is building his assembly upon a foundation that the gates of hell cannot overcome. Whatever further questions arise about Peter and the rock, the central figure of this promise is Jesus himself. He is the builder, and he is the Christ whom Peter has confessed.

But the Savior Test must include what happens next.

When Peter attempts to prevent Jesus from going to the cross, Jesus tells him:

> "Thou savourest not the things that be of God, but those that be of men." — Matthew 16:23

Here Jesus reveals the danger that remains even after a genuine spiritual awakening. Peter has received the truth of who Jesus is, but he is still interpreting the future according to what seems desirable to the natural mind. He can recognize Christ and yet resist the very path Christ has chosen.

The disciple must therefore examine not only what he believes about Jesus, but whether he is willing to follow what Jesus actually says.

Jesus gives the final test:

> "If any man will come after me, let him deny himself, and take up his cross, and follow me." — Matthew 16:24

The revelation of Christ must lead to discipleship. Peter cannot remain merely the man who made the great confession. He must become the man who follows Jesus, even when the way ahead contradicts his expectations.

This brings us to the question Jesus asks:

> "For what is a man profited, if he shall gain the whole world, and lose his own soul?" — Matthew 16:26

The disciple may gain recognition, possessions, religious authority, or the approval of others, yet lose sight of the very life Christ has come to give him. He must learn what is worth keeping and what must be surrendered.

The Savior Test brings the chapter into focus. Jesus asks the disciple to recognize who he is, receive what the Father reveals, and then follow him rather than attempting to direct him according to human expectations.

Peter's experience shows that revelation and surrender belong together. He has recognized the Christ, but he must still learn the way of the cross.

The disciple must ask himself whether he is following Jesus as Jesus truly is, or whether he is trying to make Jesus follow the path he would prefer.

The rock is revealed. The keys are given. But the disciple must still take up his cross and follow.
