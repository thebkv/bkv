LEVITICUS 01

The Fire on the Altar

BKV Insight

Leviticus 1 is about what it means to give yourself completely to God.

For the serious disciple of Jesus, this chapter gives you one of Scripture’s clearest pictures of whole-life surrender. The burnt offering is not merely placed before God and then partly returned to the worshipper. The entire offering belongs to Him. It is placed upon the altar and consumed.

That distinction will help you read the chapter.

As you study the details—the animal brought willingly, the hand placed upon its head, the blood, the washing, the cutting apart, and finally the whole offering upon the fire—keep your attention on what kind of offering this is.

Nothing is being held back.

This is something the disciple eventually discovers about following Christ. We may begin by wanting God to help us with particular parts of our lives. But the deeper call is to present the life itself to Him.

Paul uses remarkably similar language:

“present your bodies a living sacrifice, holy, acceptable unto God”
— Romans 12:1

Leviticus 1 gives us the ancient Scriptural architecture behind that idea.

The chapter can therefore help you examine your own discipleship. There are parts of ourselves we readily give to God, and parts we would prefer to manage ourselves. The burnt offering places the whole creature upon the altar.

That is the picture to carry through the chapter:

A life that once belonged to the worshipper is willingly presented wholly to God.

Know and Notice

Notice that the offering is brought “of his own voluntary will” and presented “before the LORD.”

Also notice how deliberately the chapter accounts for the whole animal. Even the inward parts and legs are washed and brought under the order of the offering.

The completeness matters.

Ask Yourself

What would it actually mean for my whole life to belong to God?

Is there anything I am willing to bring near to God, but unwilling to place upon the altar?

Canonical History & Context

Leviticus begins exactly where Exodus leaves us.

Israel has been delivered from Egypt. The covenant has been established at Sinai. The Tabernacle has been built according to the pattern shown to Moses.

Then:

“the glory of the LORD filled the tabernacle.”
— Exodus 40:34

God is dwelling in the midst of Israel.

Leviticus begins with the LORD speaking to Moses from the Tabernacle.

The great problem of Exodus was bondage: How can Israel be brought out?

Leviticus now deals with life near God.

The burnt offering is the first instruction given.

Burnt offerings were already known in Scripture. Noah offered burnt offerings after leaving the ark. Abraham was commanded to offer Isaac as a burnt offering before God provided the ram.

Here the offering becomes part of the ordered worship of Israel.

Three kinds of offerings are permitted: cattle, sheep or goats, and birds. Their value differs, but the essential meaning remains the same.

The offering is brought before the LORD and given completely upon the altar.

Natural Reading

Verses 1–2 — The LORD Speaks from the Tabernacle

The LORD calls to Moses from the Tabernacle and gives instructions concerning offerings.

The Tabernacle is no longer merely something Israel has built.

God is there.

For the disciple, that is the setting for everything that follows in Leviticus. Scripture is showing what happens when the life is ordered around the Presence of God.

⸻

Verses 3–5 — The Offering Is Brought Near

If the offering comes from the herd, the worshipper brings a male without blemish to the door of the Tabernacle.

He places his hand upon its head. The animal is accepted for him and killed before the LORD. The priests bring the blood to the altar.

The worshipper is personally involved.

He comes before God with something that belongs to him and willingly gives it up.

This is why the burnt offering is such a useful picture for the disciple. Surrender cannot be done at a distance. The life being offered is your own.

Paul later writes:

“yield yourselves unto God”
— Romans 6:13

Leviticus gives us a physical picture of that yielding.

⸻

Verses 6–9 — The Whole Offering Goes Upon the Fire

The animal is skinned and divided into its parts.

The priests arrange the pieces upon the altar. The inward parts and legs are washed with water.

Then the priest burns all of it.

This is the defining feature of the burnt offering.

Nothing is reserved for the worshipper.

Even the inward parts are brought under the cleansing and order of the sacrifice.

For the disciple, the picture reaches beyond outward religious behavior. God is concerned with the inward life as well as the visible one.

The thoughts, desires, motives, work, relationships, plans, body, and future all belong within a life offered to God.

The chapter does not require us to invent a separate symbolic meaning for every piece of the animal.

The larger picture is already powerful enough:

The whole offering belongs to God.

⸻

Verses 10–13 — The Offering from the Flock

A worshipper may also bring a sheep or goat.

Again it must be without blemish. Again it is killed before the LORD. Again the blood is brought to the altar, the animal is divided, the inward parts are washed, and the whole offering is burned.

The repetition is important.

The animal has changed.

The meaning has not.

The disciple should notice this whenever Scripture repeats an action using different materials or circumstances. The changing details can help reveal the governing function.

Here the governing function remains complete surrender.

Whether the offering is a bull or a sheep, the whole offering goes to God.

⸻

Verses 14–17 — The Offering of Birds

The final provision is for someone bringing turtledoves or young pigeons.

This is a much smaller offering.

Yet God receives it according to the same basic pattern.

The bird is brought to the altar and offered before the LORD.

This matters because Leviticus does not make nearness to God the privilege of the wealthy.

A person who cannot bring a bull may bring from the flock.

A person who cannot bring from the flock may bring birds.

The size of the offering changes according to the means of the worshipper.

The call to offer oneself to God does not.

For the disciple, this keeps surrender from becoming a comparison with someone else’s life. God is not asking you to bring another person’s resources, abilities, calling, or circumstances.

You bring your life.

And what is brought is given to Him.

Fractal Notes & References

The burnt offering belongs to a pattern that runs through Scripture: what belongs to God is given wholly to God.

Abraham and Isaac — Genesis 22

Abraham is told to offer Isaac as a burnt offering.

At the decisive moment, God provides the ram.

The story joins surrender and divine provision in a way that will continue throughout Scripture.

The Continual Burnt Offering — Exodus 29:38–46

Israel is commanded to maintain regular burnt offerings at the Tabernacle.

The passage ends with God’s promise to dwell among His people.

Sacrifice and dwelling are therefore already connected before Leviticus begins.

Jesus — Ephesians 5:2

Paul describes Christ as the one who:

“hath given himself for us an offering and a sacrifice to God”

In Christ, the pattern reaches its fullness.

He does not merely bring something to God.

He gives himself.

The Disciple — Romans 12:1

Then the same pattern appears in the life of the disciple:

“present your bodies a living sacrifice”

The sacrifice is now living.

The altar has become the shape of a life surrendered to God.

This makes Leviticus 1 much closer to Christian discipleship than it can first appear.

The animals, blood, priests, and altar belong to Israel’s actual worship.

But the governing movement continues:

A life that belongs to God is presented to God.

The Meaning of Names and Words

Leviticus

The English name Leviticus comes through Greek and Latin and refers to matters associated with the Levites.

The Hebrew name of the book comes from its opening word:

Vayikra — “And he called.”

That is a fitting beginning.

God has come to dwell among His people, and now He calls from the Tabernacle.

Offering — Qorban

The Hebrew word translated “offering” in Leviticus 1 is qorban, from a root associated with coming or bringing near.

That helps us hear the chapter correctly.

The offering is about approach to God.

Burnt Offering — Olah

The burnt offering is the Hebrew olah, related to the idea of going up or ascending.

The offering is consumed upon the altar and its smoke rises.

So even the Hebrew name preserves the visible movement of the sacrifice.

The worshipper brings the offering near.

It is given completely to God.

And it ascends from the altar.

For the disciple, Leviticus begins with a searching question:

What happens when the life that has been brought near to God is finally given entirely to Him?
