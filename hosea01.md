# HOSEA 1

## 01 — THE HOUSE OF THREE NAMES

## The Natural Reading

> "Blessed are the peacemakers: for they shall be called the children of God." — Matthew 5:9

Hosea begins with an unusual command from God. The prophet is told to take a wife of whoredoms, because the land has departed from the Lord and gone after other gods. Hosea marries Gomer, the daughter of Diblaim, and the children born into this household are given names that announce what is happening between God and Israel. The prophet's family becomes a living picture of a broken covenant. What Israel has been doing toward God is now shown through the most intimate human relationship, where love and faithfulness should have been found.

The disciple should understand why this picture is so powerful. Marriage is a relationship in which two people are bound together by a promise of faithfulness. Israel had entered into covenant with the Lord, yet had begun looking to other gods for the blessings and security that belonged to that relationship. This is more than a mistake in religious practice. It is a betrayal of trust. The disciple may recognize something similar when he claims to depend upon God while continually turning elsewhere for the things he most desires. He may speak of faithfulness while allowing some other affection, ambition, or fear to govern his decisions.

The first child is a son, and God commands Hosea to name him Jezreel. This name recalls the bloodshed associated with the house of Jehu and announces judgment upon that royal dynasty. The Lord says he will cause the kingdom of Israel to cease and break its bow in the valley of Jezreel. The northern kingdom has become powerful, but the strength upon which it relies cannot preserve it from the consequences of its conduct.

The disciple should recognize that the name brings Israel's history into the present. The deeds of an earlier generation have not simply disappeared because the kingdom has continued to prosper. There are consequences that remain to be faced. A man may establish a comfortable life around something that began in violence, dishonesty, or disobedience, and the passing of time may persuade him that everything has been settled. But God sees the foundation as well as the building. The prophet is warning Israel that its outward strength cannot guarantee its safety.

Then Gomer bears a daughter, and God commands that she be named Lo-ruhamah, meaning *Not Pitied* or *No Mercy*. The Lord declares that he will no longer continue to show Israel the mercy by which the nation has been preserved. Yet he says that he will have mercy upon the house of Judah and save them by the Lord their God, rather than by bow, sword, battle, horses, or horsemen.

This contrast is important. Israel has been trusting in its own strength, but God reminds Judah that deliverance belongs to him. The disciple should consider how easily he begins to measure his security by what he possesses or what he believes he can accomplish. He may have received mercy many times without recognizing how much he has depended upon it. The warning of Lo-ruhamah exposes the danger of treating God's patience as though it were something he could presume upon forever.

After the daughter is weaned, Gomer bears another son. God tells Hosea to name him Lo-ammi, meaning *Not My People*. The Lord declares, "For ye are not my people, and I will not be your God."

This is the most severe name of all. The covenant relationship itself is being called into question. Israel has continued to bear the name of God's people while living in a way that contradicts that identity. The Lord now confronts them with the seriousness of what they have done.

Here the disciple must examine the difference between claiming to belong to God and actually living in fellowship with him. A man may have inherited a religious tradition, learned the language of faith, and become familiar with the Scriptures. Yet none of these things should become an excuse to neglect the relationship they are meant to serve. The people of Israel still possessed their history and their identity, but Hosea was announcing that their unfaithfulness had brought them into judgment.

Then, at the moment when the chapter seems to have reached its darkest point, the prophecy changes.

God declares that the number of the children of Israel will be as the sand of the sea, which cannot be measured or numbered. And in the very place where it had been said that they were not his people, they will be called the sons of the living God.

This is a remarkable promise. The name Lo-ammi had announced the loss of covenant belonging, but God now speaks of a future in which that belonging will be restored. The judgment is real, yet it is followed by a promise of life that the people could never have secured through their own strength.

The disciple should pay attention to where the change begins. Israel has not yet accomplished some great act of restoration. The people have not suddenly become faithful. God himself announces the future he intends to bring about. The same Lord who exposes the broken covenant also speaks of the day when those who were called strangers to him will be recognized as his children.

This offers hope to the disciple who has wandered from what he once knew. He may have spent years following desires that left him far from the life he believed God was calling him to live. He may have come to think of his failures as the final account of who he is. But Hosea reveals that God can speak a new future over a people whose old way has ended in judgment. The disciple must not use that promise to excuse his unfaithfulness, but he can receive it as a reason to return.

The chapter ends with another promise. The children of Judah and the children of Israel will be gathered together and appoint themselves one head. The divided people will be united again, and the day of Jezreel will be great.

The name that first announced judgment now appears in a promise of restoration. Jezreel also carries the sense of God's sowing. The place associated with bloodshed and the breaking of Israel's strength is now connected with the future gathering of God's people. The chapter begins with the consequences of a broken covenant and ends with the prospect of a people brought together again.

The disciple should recognize the movement. God exposes the unfaithfulness that has entered the relationship, announces the consequences that must be faced, and then reveals that his purpose reaches beyond the present condition of the people. The restoration he promises is greater than anything Israel could have accomplished through its own power.

Hosea's household bears three names that tell Israel the truth about its condition. Jezreel announces judgment, Lo-ruhamah warns of mercy withdrawn, and Lo-ammi declares the broken relationship. Yet before the chapter ends, God has already spoken of a day when the rejected will be called his children and the divided people will be gathered together.

The disciple must learn to hear both parts of the message. He cannot dismiss the seriousness of turning away from God, but neither should he overlook the mercy already appearing in God's promise to restore his people.

## The Savior Test

Hosea gives us an extraordinary promise:

> "And it shall come to pass, that in the place where it was said unto them, Ye are not my people, there it shall be said unto them, Ye are the sons of the living God." — Hosea 1:10

Jesus reveals how a man who has been lost can be received again when he tells the parable of the prodigal son.

The younger son leaves his father's house, wastes his inheritance, and eventually finds himself in a condition of terrible poverty. When he comes to himself, he decides to return, although he no longer considers himself worthy to be called a son.

But Jesus tells us what happens when the father sees him:

> "But when he was yet a great way off, his father saw him, and had compassion, and ran, and fell on his neck, and kissed him." — Luke 15:20

This is a powerful confirmation of the mercy toward which Hosea is pointing. The son has genuinely abandoned his father's household, and his actions have brought him into misery. Yet when he returns, the father receives him with compassion.

The disciple should recognize that the father does not pretend the son never departed. The restoration is meaningful because the separation was real. Jesus is showing us the heart of the Father toward the one who has been lost and returns.

Jesus also tells us why he has come:

> "For the Son of man is come to seek and to save that which was lost." — Luke 19:10

Hosea describes a people who have wandered from their covenant with God. Jesus declares that his mission is to seek and save the lost. The disciple can therefore read Hosea's promise of restoration in the light of the Savior who has come to accomplish the work of reconciliation.

There is another important connection.

Hosea ends the chapter by promising that Judah and Israel will be gathered together under one head. Jesus speaks of his own work as the Shepherd:

> "And other sheep I have, which are not of this fold: them also I must bring, and they shall hear my voice; and there shall be one fold, and one shepherd." — John 10:16

Jesus is speaking of bringing other sheep into his flock, beyond those already gathered. Hosea speaks of the divided people of Israel being reunited. The promises have their own historical settings, but both reveal God's purpose to gather those who have been separated.

The disciple should see that the work of Christ reaches beyond merely correcting the outward behavior of individuals. Jesus is bringing people into fellowship with the Father and gathering them under his care.

Jesus also speaks directly of becoming children of God:

> "Blessed are the peacemakers: for they shall be called the children of God." — Matthew 5:9

In Hosea, those once declared not to be God's people are promised a future in which they will be called his sons. Jesus teaches his disciples to live as children of the Father, bearing the character of the One to whom they belong.

This is important because the restored relationship must become visible in the life. The disciple cannot simply claim the name of God's child while continuing to live according to the very impulses that led him away. He is being called to receive the Father's life and allow it to change the way he treats others.

The New Testament also gives us an explicit interpretation of Hosea's promise. Paul writes:

> "I will call them my people, which were not my people; and her beloved, which was not beloved." — Romans 9:25

And Peter tells believers:

> "Which in time past were not a people, but are now the people of God: which had not obtained mercy, but now have obtained mercy." — 1 Peter 2:10

These passages show how the apostles understood Hosea's promise in relation to the people being gathered through Jesus Christ. The language of rejected and restored belonging becomes part of their explanation of the gospel's reach.

The Savior Test brings the chapter into focus. Hosea exposes the unfaithfulness of God's people and announces the judgment that follows. Yet the chapter ends with a promise that those who had lost their place will once again be called the children of the living God.

Jesus reveals the Father's compassion toward the lost, declares his mission to seek and save them, and gathers his sheep under one Shepherd.

The disciple should ask himself whether he has been living according to the relationship he claims to have with God. Has something else taken the place of his first devotion? Has he become so familiar with the outward identity of faith that he has neglected the life to which it calls him?

And if he has wandered, will he receive the invitation to return?

Hosea begins with a household whose names announce the broken covenant. But God speaks of a future in which those names will give way to mercy and restored belonging.

Jesus shows the disciple the Father's willingness to receive the one who comes home.

The people who were called *Not My People* will be called *the sons of the living God*.
