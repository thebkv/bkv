# RUTH 04

## THE SANDAL AT THE GATE

*Redemption Completed in the Light*

---

## BKV Insight

Ruth 4 brings the promise of the threshing floor into the daylight. Boaz has received Ruth’s request and pledged himself to act, but another kinsman has the first right to redeem. Before the household can be restored, that claim must be faced and settled.

So Boaz goes to the gate of Bethlehem. He calls the nearer kinsman aside and gathers ten elders as witnesses. The matter that Ruth entrusted to him in the night is now his responsibility to carry through in public.

At first, the nearer man agrees to redeem Elimelech’s land. Then Boaz explains what belongs to the decision. Ruth is involved, and with her the responsibility of preserving the dead man’s name upon his inheritance. The man withdraws:

> “I cannot redeem it for myself, lest I mar mine own inheritance.” — Ruth 4:6

His reason reveals the difference between the two men. He is willing to acquire the land, but the fuller obligation may endanger what he already has. Boaz is willing to accept that obligation and carry another household’s future forward.

The nearer kinsman releases his right. Boaz accepts the responsibility. The sandal changes hands.

What Boaz promised at the threshing floor can now be established before witnesses. The land, the family name, Ruth’s future, and the continuation of the household are being dealt with together.

The disciple should pause over the completeness of this responsibility. We often come to God with the need that presses most heavily upon us. We ask for bread, protection, relief, or guidance. As His work continues, we discover other matters that also need to come into His care: attachments we have defended, habits we have excused, and decisions over which we still insist on having the final word.

Ruth’s story gives us a searching way to consider that resistance. The nearer kinsman measures the obligation against the inheritance he wants to preserve. We too may welcome God’s provision while remaining unwilling to yield the arrangement of life we have made for ourselves. We want His help, yet hesitate when trusting Him requires something to change.

The account at the gate remains a real family transaction involving a real prior claim. Its inward application asks a question of us: **how much of the life we have brought to God are we still trying to keep under our own government?**

Ruth has moved through several stages to reach this point. She chose Naomi’s people and God, entered the field, received provision, and asked for covering. Now the remaining claim is settled, and Boaz publicly accepts the responsibility that will allow her to enter his household.

The story then becomes fruitful. Boaz marries Ruth, the LORD gives her conception, and a son is born. Naomi, who came home saying she had been brought back empty, receives the child into her arms. Her losses remain part of her history, but a future has opened where she could once see only an ending.

The child is Obed, the father of Jesse and grandfather of David. The redemption of one household has become part of the preparation for Israel’s king.

**RETURN → PROVISION → COVERING → REDEMPTION → FRUITFULNESS**

For the disciple, this final chapter gathers the journey into a call to deeper trust. We may bring the whole matter before God, including what we fear surrendering and what we cannot repair ourselves. His care reaches beyond the need that first brought us to Him. A life restored under His care can become fruitful in ways its owner could never have planned.

## The Savior Test

Boaz’s willingness at the gate helps the disciple recognize something of the character Jesus reveals in its fullness. Another man has declined the obligation; Boaz accepts it and acts for the good of a household whose future is in danger.

Jesus describes His own purpose in these words:

> “For even the Son of man came not to be ministered unto, but to minister, and to give his life a ransom for many.” — Mark 10:45

The comparison has its proper limits. Boaz redeems within Israel’s family and property arrangements; Christ gives Himself for the salvation of men. Yet Boaz’s readiness to bear a costly responsibility directs our attention toward the Savior’s willing self-giving.

Jesus speaks with the same freedom of love when He says:

> “I am the good shepherd: the good shepherd giveth his life for the sheep.” — John 10:11

The disciple is learning whom he may trust. The Redeemer receives people whose restoration is costly, and His willingness is shown in what He gives for them. We need not conceal the depth of our need from the One who has already come to save.

Ruth 4 also shows a promise being carried through. Boaz leaves the threshing floor and goes to the place where he can settle the matter. His kindness endures through the responsibility of completion.

Jesus speaks of His own obedience to the Father:

> “I have glorified thee on the earth: I have finished the work which thou gavest me to do.” — John 17:4

And on the cross He declares:

> “It is finished.” — John 19:30

The disciple’s confidence rests in the Savior who accomplishes the work entrusted to Him. Ruth could make her request, but she could not settle the claim at the gate herself. Boaz had the standing and willingness to do what lay beyond her power. Her waiting rested on his promise and character.

This gives us a useful distinction in our own life of faith. There is obedience that belongs to us, and there is a work we must entrust to Christ. We may answer His call, yield what He exposes, and act on the truth He gives us. Our confidence in redemption rests upon Him.

Then Ruth’s household receives new life. The women speak to Naomi of the child as:

> “a restorer of thy life, and a nourisher of thine old age.” — Ruth 4:15

Ruth has found a home, Naomi receives comfort, and the family has a future. The legal settlement has opened into a living relationship and the care of a child.

Jesus tells us the purpose of His coming:

> “I am come that they might have life, and that they might have it more abundantly.” — John 10:10

In Christ, the disciple is brought into fellowship with the Father and called to live from that relationship. The fruit of redemption appears in the life that follows: in love, faithfulness, obedience, and the ability to nourish others through what God has given.

The book’s final genealogy makes its connection with Christ still more direct. Obed becomes the father of Jesse, and Jesse the father of David. Matthew later names Ruth in the generations of Jesus Christ:

> “And Booz begat Obed of Ruth; and Obed begat Jesse.” — Matthew 1:5

The Moabite widow who chose to accompany Naomi belongs within the human ancestry of the Messiah. She could not have seen that future on the road from Moab. Boaz could not have measured the full reach of his decision at the gate. Their faithfulness served a purpose that extended far beyond their sight.

The Savior Test therefore brings us both assurance and examination. Christ reveals the willing Redeemer who gives Himself, fulfills His work, and brings life to those He saves. The disciple may trust Him with the whole condition of his life. He must also consider where he has welcomed provision while resisting the change that a deeper surrender would require.

Ruth came seeking refuge beneath the wings of the LORD. Through the faithfulness of Boaz, she received a household and a future. Through the generations that followed, the Redeemer Himself came into the world.

## Canonical History & Context

### The City Gate

The gate of an ancient town served as a public place for community business, witnessed agreements, disputes, and decisions involving the elders. It is the appropriate setting for the responsibility Boaz has promised to undertake.

At the threshing floor, Ruth made a private request. At the gate, Boaz brings the matter before the nearer kinsman and ten elders so that the arrangement can be recognized by the community. The witnesses give public standing to what he is about to declare.

### The Nearer Kinsman

The unnamed relative has a genuine prior right, which Boaz honors. He initially agrees to redeem the land, then declines when the fuller responsibility is explained. His stated concern is the effect upon his own inheritance.

The story gives us enough to understand his decision without making him a villain. His refusal also helps us see what Boaz accepts. Redeeming this possession carries obligations toward Ruth, the dead man’s name, and the future of the family connected to the land.

### Land and Family

Leviticus 25 provides for a near relative to redeem family property when poverty has forced its sale. Deuteronomy 25 addresses the continuation of a deceased man’s name through his brother. Ruth 4 brings the concerns of property, widowhood, family name, and inheritance together in its own particular circumstances.

Boaz and the nearer kinsman are not presented as Mahlon’s brothers, so this is not a straightforward application of the levirate marriage law. The broader family responsibilities help us understand the case, while the details of Ruth’s account should be allowed to stand on their own.

### The Sandal

Ruth explains the custom directly:

> “Now this was the manner in former time in Israel concerning redeeming and concerning changing, for to confirm all things; a man plucked off his shoe, and gave it to his neighbour: and this was a testimony in Israel.” — Ruth 4:7

The nearer kinsman removes his sandal to confirm the transfer of his right. The action resembles the sandal ceremony in Deuteronomy 25, but the circumstances and details differ. Deuteronomy describes the widow removing the refusing brother-in-law’s sandal, with spitting and a public declaration of shame. Ruth describes a recognized custom confirming redemption and exchange.

The sandal is a physical testimony to the agreement. The prior claim has been released, and Boaz can proceed before the witnesses.

## Natural Reading

### Ruth 4:1–2 — Boaz Goes to the Gate

> “Then went Boaz up to the gate, and sat him down there.”

Boaz calls the nearer kinsman aside and gathers ten elders. Naomi ended the previous chapter confident that he would not rest until he had finished the matter that day. The opening of Chapter 4 shows him acting on that responsibility.

Ruth had been told to wait because the next step belonged to Boaz. He now goes where the matter can be settled. His promise has become a course of action.

For the disciple, there is reassurance in this movement. Ruth’s waiting is grounded in the character of the person she has trusted. The work continues beyond what she can do herself.

### Ruth 4:3–4 — The Land Is Offered

Boaz presents the matter of Elimelech’s land and acknowledges the other man’s first right. He asks him to redeem before the inhabitants and elders, explaining that Boaz stands next if the man declines.

The nearer kinsman answers, “I will redeem it.” He has been given a genuine opportunity, and the witnesses hear his response.

Boaz’s conduct shows the integrity within his willingness. His promise to Ruth commits him to facing the obstacle properly. The disciple may learn from that patience: a good purpose must be pursued with care for the responsibilities and rights already involved.

### Ruth 4:5 — The Full Responsibility Is Revealed

Boaz explains that the transaction also concerns Ruth, the widow of the dead, and the purpose of raising up the dead man’s name upon his inheritance.

The land belongs to the history and future of a household. To redeem it in these circumstances is to accept an obligation toward the people connected with it. This fuller explanation changes the nearer kinsman’s decision.

The disciple should notice the reach of the responsibility Boaz is prepared to bear. He has seen the widows’ immediate hunger, but he is willing to address the deeper loss that the provision of grain alone could not repair.

### Ruth 4:6 — The Nearer Kinsman Declines

> “I cannot redeem it for myself, lest I mar mine own inheritance: redeem thou my right to thyself; for I cannot redeem it.”

The nearer man releases his right because of the risk he sees to his own inheritance. Boaz is willing to accept what that man declines.

The contrast is clear enough without speculation about the unnamed man’s motives beyond what he states. One man withdraws to protect his existing inheritance; the other accepts the responsibility required to restore a threatened household.

As an inward application, the scene asks how we respond when faithfulness reaches beyond what is convenient or advantageous to ourselves. Willingness becomes clear when the fuller obligation is known.

### Ruth 4:7–8 — The Sandal Changes Hands

The narrator explains the sandal custom, and the nearer kinsman tells Boaz, “Buy it for thee.” He removes his shoe to confirm the arrangement.

The obstacle carried over from Chapter 3 has now been resolved. Boaz is free to act, and the transfer is witnessed.

A simple physical action marks a real change. The promise made at the threshing floor has reached the point where it can be publicly established.

### Ruth 4:9–10 — Boaz Redeems

> “Ye are witnesses this day.”

Boaz declares before the elders and people that he has acquired what belonged to Elimelech, Chilion, and Mahlon. He also names Ruth as the woman who will become his wife, explaining his intention to preserve the dead man’s name upon his inheritance.

He accepts the connected responsibilities openly. Land, marriage, family name, and continuity are joined in the declaration.

Ruth’s future now rests on more than a private assurance. The matter has been brought before the community, and Boaz has publicly committed himself to carry it through.

### Ruth 4:11–12 — The People Bless the House

The elders and people answer, “We are witnesses.” They bless Ruth’s entrance into the household by invoking Rachel and Leah, who built the house of Israel, and Perez, whom Tamar bore to Judah.

The words reach back into Israel’s family history as they look toward this household’s future. Tamar’s story also concerns widowhood and a threatened line within Judah, giving the blessing a particular connection with the responsibility Boaz has accepted.

Ruth entered Bethlehem as a foreign widow. The community now blesses her as a woman coming into an Israelite household. Her declaration that Naomi’s people would be her people is receiving a public answer.

### Ruth 4:13 — The LORD Gives Conception

> “So Boaz took Ruth, and she was his wife.”

The narrator then tells us that “the LORD gave her conception, and she bare a son.” The household threatened by death now receives a child.

The legal settlement has opened into marriage and fruitfulness. The birth is expressly attributed to the LORD, whose provision has been unfolding throughout the story.

### Ruth 4:14–15 — A Restorer of Thy Life

The women bless the LORD and tell Naomi that the child will be “a restorer of thy life, and a nourisher of thine old age.” They also praise Ruth, whose love has proved better to Naomi than seven sons.

The story returns our attention to the woman who came home calling herself empty. New life has come into the household, and Ruth’s place in that restoration is now plain to everyone.

Part of the answer had been walking beside Naomi before she could recognize it. The daughter-in-law she once urged to return to Moab has remained faithful all the way into this new beginning.

### Ruth 4:16–17 — A Son Is Born to Naomi

Naomi takes the child into her bosom and becomes his caregiver. The women say, “There is a son born to Naomi,” and name him Obed.

Obed is the son of Ruth and Boaz. The women’s words express what his birth means for Naomi’s household: the family has a living future again.

Her husband and sons have not returned, and their deaths remain real. She is nevertheless holding a child whose life continues the family story. Restoration has given her something she could not see when she named herself bitter and empty.

### Ruth 4:17 — Obed, Jesse, David

> “he is the father of Jesse, the father of David.”

With these names, the story opens beyond the household. Obed will become the grandfather of David, and this family’s restoration belongs within the history of Israel’s kingship.

Ruth chose Naomi before she could know those generations. Boaz showed kindness in the field before he could measure its full consequence. Their faithfulness served a future beyond their sight.

The disciple may take courage from that wider view. We meet our duties close at hand, while their fruit may reach people and places we will never know.

### Ruth 4:18–22 — The Genealogy

The closing genealogy begins with Perez, named Pharez in the KJV, and continues through Hezron, Ram, Amminadab, Nahshon, Salmon, Boaz, Obed, Jesse, and David.

It looks backward to the ancestor invoked in the elders’ blessing and forward to the king whose ancestry the book has been tracing. The household’s restoration is placed within a continuing line.

The ending allows us to see several scales at once. Naomi receives a child, the family receives a future, and Israel receives the ancestry of David. Matthew will later carry that line onward to Jesus Christ.

## Fractal Notes & References

### 1. Famine → Harvest → Fruitfulness

Ruth begins with famine, moves through the barley and wheat harvests, and ends with conception and birth. Bethlehem, left during scarcity, becomes the place where the household receives provision and new life.

**FAMINE → HARVEST → FRUITFULNESS**

**Confidence: ESTABLISHED**

### 2. Death of the Line → Restoration of the Line

The deaths of Elimelech, Mahlon, and Chilion threaten the household’s continuation. Chapter 4 repeatedly speaks of preserving the dead man’s name upon his inheritance, then closes with a genealogy. The threatened line is carried forward through redemption.

**Confidence: ESTABLISHED**

### 3. Stranger → House

In Chapter 2, Ruth wonders why Boaz has noticed her, “seeing I am a stranger.” At the gate, the elders bless “the woman that is come into thine house.” Her changing place is explicit in the story.

**STRANGER → GLEANER → PROTECTED → REQUEST FOR COVERING → REDEEMED → HOUSE**

**Confidence: ESTABLISHED**

### 4. Emptiness → Restored Life

Naomi says she has returned empty. Boaz later sends grain with the instruction that Ruth must not go empty to her mother-in-law. In Chapter 4, Naomi holds the child whom the women call a restorer of her life. The answer unfolds in stages, and the book’s own language connects them.

**Confidence: ESTABLISHED**

### 5. Nearness → Willingness → Redemption

The nearer kinsman has the first right but declines the full obligation. Boaz is willing to act once that claim has been released. The story distinguishes having a prior place in the family arrangement from accepting the responsibility that will complete the redemption.

**Confidence: ESTABLISHED**

### 6. Inheritance → Responsibility

The nearer kinsman’s answer changes when the responsibility toward Ruth and the dead man’s name is explained. Boaz accepts that fuller obligation. The disciple may recognize a principle here: what is entrusted to us must be faithfully cared for, including the people whose lives are bound up with it.

**Confidence: STRONG RECONSTRUCTION**

### 7. Private Promise → Public Establishment

Ruth makes her request privately. Boaz promises to act, then gathers witnesses, settles the prior claim, and declares the redemption at the gate.

**THRESHING FLOOR → PROMISE → GATE → WITNESSES → ESTABLISHMENT**

**Confidence: ESTABLISHED**

### 8. Field → Threshing Floor → Gate → House

The settings follow the developing relationship. In the field, Ruth receives provision. At the threshing floor, she asks for covering. At the gate, the claim is settled. In the household, marriage becomes fruitful.

These locations retain their ordinary functions while allowing us to follow the larger movement from immediate need toward settled belonging and a continuing future.

**Confidence: STRONG RECONSTRUCTION**

### 9. Ruth → David

The Moabite widow who enters Bethlehem becomes the mother of Obed and an ancestress of David. The genealogy gives this destination explicitly, revealing how her personal faithfulness belongs within Israel’s royal history.

**Confidence: ESTABLISHED**

### 10. Judges → David

Ruth opens in the days of the judges and ends with David. Read alongside the closing declaration of Judges, that there was no king in Israel, its household story takes on a wider significance. Within the unsettled age, faithful lives are carrying the ancestry of the coming king.

**NO KING → FAITHFUL HOUSEHOLD → DAVID**

**Confidence: STRONG RECONSTRUCTION**

## Onomastics

### Ruth — רוּת

**Meaning:** Traditionally associated with friendship or companionship; the exact derivation is uncertain.

**MSFR:** The Moabite widow who cleaves to Naomi, enters Bethlehem, gleans, seeks redemption, marries Boaz, and bears Obed.

**BKV:** Her chosen belonging has become a settled place within the people she joined. The story’s force rests in her faithfulness and incorporation, independently of the proposed meaning of her name.

### Boaz — בֹּעַז / Bo’az

**Meaning:** The exact etymology is uncertain. Traditional associations with strength should remain secondary.

**MSFR:** The kinsman who provides for Ruth, receives her request, honors the nearer claim, publicly redeems, and becomes her husband and Obed’s father.

**BKV:** His character is shown through responsibility carried to completion. Protection, generosity, restraint, and lawful action give substance to his willingness.

### Naomi — נָעֳמִי / No’omi

**Meaning:** Pleasantness, pleasant, or my pleasantness.

**MSFR:** Elimelech’s widow, whose household is emptied in Moab and receives a continuing future through Ruth, Boaz, and Obed.

**BKV:** Her understanding changes as the story unfolds. She returns unable to see beyond her loss and eventually holds the child through whom the household will continue.

### Mara — מָרָא

**Meaning:** Bitter.

Naomi asks to be called Mara in Chapter 1, while the narrator continues to call her Naomi. By Chapter 4, provision, kindness, and new life have become part of the account she can give of her life.

**BKV:** Mara expresses her real sorrow at the time she speaks. The story continues beyond that moment and gives her more to receive than she could then imagine.

### Obed — עוֹבֵד / Oved

**Meaning:** Serving one or servant, from the Hebrew root עבד, *abad*, to work or serve; the root can also concern worship or service to God.

**MSFR:** The son of Ruth and Boaz, received into Naomi’s care, and the father of Jesse and grandfather of David.

**BKV:** His birth gives living expression to the restoration of the family. Through him, the household’s future opens toward the royal line.

### Jesse — יִשַׁי / Yishai

**Meaning:** Etymology uncertain.

**MSFR:** Obed’s son and David’s father.

**BKV:** His place is chiefly genealogical here, carrying the restored line one generation closer to David.

### David — דָּוִד / David

**Meaning:** Commonly associated with beloved, though precise etymological questions remain.

**MSFR:** Jesse’s son and the future king of Israel.

**BKV:** His name reveals the larger reach of the household’s restoration. The events lived in Bethlehem during the judges belong to the preparation for Israel’s kingship.

### Go’el — גֹּאֵל

**Meaning:** Redeemer; a near relative who may act to recover family property, rights, or continuity.

**MSFR:** The family role at the center of the chapter. The nearer kinsman receives the first opportunity, while Boaz becomes the one who accepts and completes the responsibility.

**BKV:** Redemption preserves what loss has threatened. Through the redeemer’s action, inheritance, family name, household, and future can continue together.

## The Chapter in One Movement

Boaz goes to the gate to finish what he promised Ruth. Before the elders, he gives the nearer kinsman the first opportunity to redeem. The man agrees to acquire the land, then withdraws when the responsibility toward Ruth and the dead man’s name becomes clear.

The sandal confirms the release of his right. Boaz declares his commitment before witnesses, and the community blesses the household that will follow. Ruth becomes his wife, the LORD gives conception, and Obed is born.

The story then returns to Naomi. She came home remembering the husband and sons she had lost. Now she receives a child whose life gives the family a future again. The women recognize Ruth’s love, bless the LORD, and speak of restored life.

Finally, the genealogy lets us see beyond what anyone in that household could yet know. Obed will become the father of Jesse, and Jesse the father of David. Matthew will later name Ruth in the ancestry of Jesus Christ.

**FAMINE → DEPARTURE → LOSS → RETURN → HARVEST → GLEANING → FAVOR → REFUGE → COVERING → PROMISE → GATE → REDEMPTION → HOUSE → FRUITFULNESS → RESTORED LINE → DAVID**

The whole journey has been carried through ordinary acts of faithfulness. Ruth stayed with Naomi. She went to work, received kindness, and made her request. Naomi began to hope again and guided her toward a settled future. Boaz accepted the responsibility and carried it through.

The disciple cannot always see what God is preparing through the duty immediately before him. He can keep faith with the person entrusted to him, bring what remains unresolved into the light, and yield his life more fully to the Savior he has learned to trust.

At the beginning, Naomi could see a household coming to its end. At the close, she holds a child. Beyond that child are generations, a king, and, in the fullness of time, the coming of Christ.

God’s purpose had been reaching farther than she could see, even while she was finding her way home.
